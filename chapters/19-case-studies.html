Worked case studies

Five real-sized projects, each taken from requirements through concept, numbers, materials and printing to the traps you will meet. Each has a small calculator to play with so you can see how the pieces interact. Nothing here introduces new theory: it is the previous eighteen chapters in use.

Case 1 · A servo-driven parallel gripper

Requirement: pick up objects up to 40 mm wide and up to 300 g with a small servo; fingers soft enough not to damage them.

Concept. The simplest mechanism for a parallel jaw is a pinion between two racks (chapter 7): the servo turns the pinion, the racks drive the two jaws in opposite directions, so the object stays centred. Each jaw rides on a printed guide (chapter 9) and carries a TPU pad for friction (chapter 3). A gripper must work with the servo's derated torque (chapter 16).

The numbers. The pinion's torque T is shared between the two racks: each jaw pushes with F = T / (2·r) (r = pinion pitch radius). Holding force needs friction on both pads: 2·μ·F ≥ SF · m·g. Opening stroke per jaw is r·θ (θ = 180° of servo travel), so a bigger pinion opens wider but grips weaker: the classic trade-off.

Calculator 1 · Gripper force and capacity

Printing. Pinion and racks in nylon or PETG at module 1.5–2, printed flat; jaws printed in the plane of the load; TPU pads glued or printed with the second nozzle. Pitfalls: a servo holding a grip continuously overheats (add a self-locking worm or a spring-loaded ratchet if holding for long); the object slips if pads are hard; backlash in the rack and pinion centres the object poorly: add a light spring across the jaws as a preload.

Case 2 · A motorised camera slider

Requirement: 1 m of smooth travel carrying a 1.5 kg camera, with time-lapse speeds as slow as 30 minutes per metre and video speeds up to 100 mm/s.

Concept. A GT2 belt loop driven by a NEMA 17 (chapter 8) pulls a carriage on two 8 mm steel rods with LM8UU bearings or a profile rail (chapter 9). The frame is a length of 2020 extrusion (chapter 15) with printed end caps, motor mount (slotted for belt tension) and carriage plate. Over-constraint is avoided by fixing one rod bearing and letting the second float.

The numbers. Travel per motor turn = teeth × pitch (20 × 2 = 40 mm), so 1 m takes 25 revolutions: at a 30-minute move the motor turns at a tiny 0.8 rpm (microstepping makes it smooth), at 100 mm/s it turns at 150 rpm (well within the NEMA 17's range, chapter 16). Belt force for acceleration is F = m·a plus a little friction: even 1 m/s² on 1.5 kg is only 1.5 N.

Calculator 2 · Slider speed, steps and motor load

Printing. Carriage plate in PETG or PA-CF, with bearing pockets sized from the fit coupon; belt clamp teeth printed fine; a stiff motor mount in PA-CF or with a metal plate behind. Pitfalls: belt flutter on a long span at certain speeds (shorten or tension up); stepper resonance at low speed (use microstepping or a TMC2209-class driver); sagging of a long unsupported rod (use a supported rail for more than ~600 mm or a thicker rod); a camera on a long arm adds a moment: keep the load centred over the guides.

Case 3 · A compact planetary gearbox

Requirement: reduce a small motor (0.05 N·m, 3000 rpm) to about 10:1 inside a 60 mm diameter, with several N·m of output torque.

Concept. A single-stage planetary (chapter 7): sun on the motor shaft, three planets on a carrier, fixed ring gear, output from the carrier. Ratio = 1 + Zr/Zs. A ratio near 10 wants Zr ≈ 9 Zs: a small sun (e.g. 10 teeth) and a large ring, so the planet size is (Zr − Zs)/2 teeth. Constraints: assembly condition (Zs + Zr)/N an integer, and the weakest gear (the sun, with few teeth) takes the full motor torque.

Calculator 3 · Planetary gearbox sizing

Printing. Herringbone or helical gears are quiet and self-aligning; print the ring gear housing with extra walls, planets and sun in nylon (or PA-CF for stiffness); carrier plates with steel pins or M3 screws and small bearings; a sun gear on a D-shaft with a grub screw. Pitfalls: ring gear teeth are tight to print at small sizes (use module ≥ 1.2); planets that don't mesh at the same time when the assembly condition fails; a floppy ring gear housing (stiffen); heat in a closed gearbox (a slip clutch and rest periods).

Case 4 · A compliant parallel clamp

Requirement: a one-piece holder that clamps a 12 mm rod (or a pen, a probe) with a steady force, released by squeezing two tabs. No pins, no screws.

Concept. A printed spring clip: two cantilever arms (chapter 12) with jaws on their free ends, a thick root and fillets. Releasing means deflecting the arms outward: strain ε = 1.5·t·y/L² (cyclic limit for PETG 1.5%, nylon 3%). Clamp force = the beam stiffness times the preload deflection.

Calculator 4 · Clamp arm strain and force

Printing. Print flat so the arms' length is in the XY plane; use 4+ walls, no infill in the arms; fillet the root (1.5 mm minimum); design for the pre-load deflection to be less than the maximum opening deflection so that it grips but is not over-stressed. Pitfalls: creep (the clip loosens over weeks if left clamped on a rod in PLA); a clamp for an out-of-round object; temperature (a warm car).

Case 5 · A Hoeken-linkage walker

Requirement: a small walking toy with one motor driving two or more legs.

Concept. A Hoeken linkage per leg (chapter 5): a crank-rocker whose foot point traces a path with a long, nearly straight bottom edge, so the body glides forward while the foot is on the ground, then lifts it back. Several legs run on one crankshaft with phase offsets (e.g. 0° and 180° for two pairs, or three legs at 120°) so that at least one set is always in contact.

Calculator 5 · Hoeken leg

The foot (orange) traces the curve; the green stretch is the nearly-straight ground contact. Change the crank radius to scale the leg, and the motor speed to see the body speed.

Printing. All links print flat in PETG or PLA at 3–4 mm thick; each pivot is an M3 screw with a bushing or a shoulder screw; spacers keep legs and links out of each other's planes; the crankshaft is steel with D-flats and grub screws. Pitfalls: the ground-contact fraction of one leg is under 50%, so you need three or more legs for stable walking; feet slip without rubber pads; link lengths must match the ratios exactly (check the CAD skeleton; a 0.5 mm error changes the path); friction in many pivots adds up: use bearings or a stronger motor.

What the five have in common

  • The critical interface was found first: the bearing seat, the gear mesh, the jaw friction. Each got a coupon.
  • The load path was designed, not left to chance: metal pins and rods where the force concentrates, printed parts around them.
  • The weak link was chosen on purpose: a slip clutch, a cheap pad, a replaceable bushing.
  • Calculations were done first, in a few lines, and the first print was a test of the calculation.

Check yourself

In the gripper, doubling the pinion radius does what to the opening stroke, the grip force and the holdable mass?

Stroke doubles, grip force halves (F = T/2r), holdable mass halves. Use a gear reduction or a leverage change if you need both width and force.

A 1 m slider needs to move in 10 minutes with a 20-tooth GT2 pulley and 16× microstepping. What step rate is that?

Speed = 1000 mm / 600 s = 1.67 mm/s. Steps per mm = 200 × 16 / 40 = 80, so the step rate = 1.67 × 80 ≈ 133 microsteps per second: trivially low for a driver, but a stepper turns only 2.5 rpm, so use microstepping (or a quiet driver) for smooth motion.

Why can't a walker with a single Hoeken leg per side walk stably?

The straight ground-contact portion is less than half of the cycle for one leg, so with only one leg per side there are moments where no leg supports the body. Use at least three legs with phase offsets, or four in two phase groups.
