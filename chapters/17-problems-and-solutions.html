Part C · Putting it together · Chapter 17

Common problems and proven solutions

Almost every printed mechanism fails in one of a few familiar ways, and almost every failure has a standard remedy. This chapter is the lookup table: a searchable list of symptoms with causes and fixes (and the chapter where each is explained), then three calculators for the problems that are really about numbers: tolerance stack-up, thermal expansion and stick-slip.

17.1 A way of debugging

  1. Isolate. Find the smallest assembly that shows the problem. Remove the motor, the belt, the cover. Does the carriage move freely by hand? Does the gear turn alone?
  2. Measure, don't guess. Calipers on the part, a scale for forces, a phone camera slow-motion for motion, a thermometer or fingertip for heat.
  3. Classify. Is it a geometry problem (fit, alignment, over-constraint), a material problem (creep, brittleness, wear), a load problem (too much torque, too fast), or a process problem (warp, shrink, bad layers)?
  4. Change one thing at a time. A different material and a different clearance together teach you nothing.
  5. Print a test piece, not the whole part. Most fixes can be checked on a 10-minute coupon.
  6. Write it down. A line in a notebook ("608 seat, PETG, 0.15 shrink → snug") is worth more than a hundred remembered attempts (chapter 18).

17.2 The troubleshooter

Type a word (bind, crack, loose, noise, heat...) or pick a category.

17.3 Tolerance stack-up

A gap is the sum of several dimensions, each with its own tolerance. Printed dimensions are typically good to ±0.1–0.2 mm, bought parts to ±0.02–0.1 mm. In a chain of several, the errors add up. Two ways to add them:

  • Worst case: every part at its extreme in the worst direction at once: ±Σ tol. Safe, but conservative.
  • Root-sum-square (RSS): statistical: ±√(Σ tol²). Realistic if the errors are independent; roughly what you will actually see across many assemblies.

Design the gap so that the worst-case minimum still works (for safety-critical or one-off parts), or the RSS minimum (for large batches where a rare failure is OK). The simulation draws both and runs a Monte-Carlo of 4000 random assemblies.

17.4 Thermal expansion

Everything grows with temperature: ΔL = α · L · ΔT. Plastics expand 3–8× more than steel, so a printed part carrying a steel rod, or sharing a bolt with a metal plate, gets looser when cold and tighter when warm. This matters for long parts (100 mm of PLA grows 0.07 mm per 10 K), for snug fits that must hold at both ends of a temperature range, and for plastic parts clamped to metal.

17.5 Stick-slip

When a part slides slowly against a surface whose static friction is higher than its kinetic friction, and it is driven through something springy, it moves in jerks: it sticks, the drive winds up, it breaks free and lurches ahead, then sticks again. This is the squeal of a door hinge, the judder of a slow slide, the "grunting" of a leadscrew axis. The cure is one of: lower the static-to-kinetic ratio (lubricant, a better material pair), a stiffer drive, or a different speed.

17.6 The ten fixes that solve most problems

  1. Add a fillet to every inside corner that carries load.
  2. Reorient the part so loads run along layers.
  3. Print a fit coupon for each critical interface before the real part.
  4. Put a metal part where metal belongs: shaft, pin, bearing, insert, spring.
  5. Make one guide fixed and the other floating to avoid over-constraint.
  6. Increase the guide span or reduce the push offset (a ≥ L/2μ jams).
  7. Lengthen the flexure or thin it to bring the strain down.
  8. Add a washer, a shoulder or a cover so soft plastic isn't relied on to clamp or retain.
  9. Give moving parts a lubricant and a different material from their partner.
  10. Make it adjustable (a slot, a shim, a turnbuckle) wherever tolerances can't be held.

17.7 Check yourself

Four independent dimensions each have a tolerance of ±0.1 mm. What are the worst-case and RSS stack-up?

Worst case: ±0.4 mm. RSS: √(4 × 0.1²) = ±0.2 mm. Half of the worst case, and it is the number you would see most of the time.

A 150 mm PLA frame (α ≈ 68 µm/m·K) holds a steel rod (12 µm/m·K) in a snug hole. The workshop warms by 20 K. How much does the fit change?

Δ = (68 − 12) × 10⁻⁶ × 150 × 20 = 0.17 mm. A snug fit becomes loose when it warms (the plastic hole grows more than the rod) and tight when it cools; plan a slip fit for long spans.

A sliding axis judders at slow speed but is smooth when run fast. Why, and what is the cheapest fix?

Stick-slip: static friction exceeds kinetic friction and the drive is springy, and slow speeds give the mass time to stick. Cheapest fix: lubricant (lowers both, and narrows the gap), or run faster; longer term, a stiffer drive and a better material pair.
