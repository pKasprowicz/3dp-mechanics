Fasteners, joints and springs

Most printed assemblies are held together by a few screws, a few press fits and a few springs, and most disappointments come from how those are done: a screw that strips on the third tightening, a joint that crushes under the head, a spring that sags. This chapter is the practical catalogue: what to use, what size to model it, how tight to go, and how to pick the spring you buy.

13.2 Machine screws in printed parts

For metric screws the useful numbers are the thread size, the clearance hole that a screw passes through, the head and nut sizes (for counterbores and nut pockets), and the sizes for inserts. Printed holes come out small (chapter 4), so always add your measured hole shrink to anything that must fit a bought part.

  • Clearance holes (ISO medium series): M2 2.4, M2.5 2.9, M3 3.4, M4 4.5, M5 5.5, M6 6.6, M8 9 mm. Printed holes need the shrink added on top.
  • Counterbore for a socket-head cap screw: head diameter + 0.4 to 0.6 mm wide, head height + 0.3 mm deep. The head is about 1.5× the thread diameter (M3: 5.5 mm).
  • Captive nut pocket: across-flats of the nut (M3: 5.5 mm) + 0.2–0.4 mm; depth = nut thickness + 0.2 mm. Add a side slot to slide the nut in, or pause the print and drop it in before it is bridged over.
  • Direct threading into plastic works for M3 and larger in light-duty cases: print a pilot hole about 80% of the screw diameter (M3: ≈ 2.5 mm) and drive a thread-forming screw. Expect it to loosen after a handful of cycles.

Heat-set inserts

A brass insert with a knurled outside is pressed, hot, into a slightly smaller printed hole with a soldering iron (with a tip made for inserts). The plastic melts around the knurls and sets, giving a metal thread that survives repeated screwing. This is by far the best way to get a durable thread in a printed part.

  • Hole diameter is a little smaller than the insert's outside diameter (typically 0.4–0.6 mm smaller). Check the insert maker's datasheet: M3 inserts of ~4.6 mm OD usually want a ~4.0 mm hole.
  • Hole depth ≥ insert length + 1 mm, to give displaced plastic somewhere to go and to prevent bottoming.
  • Wall around the hole ≥ 1.5 mm, ideally 2+ mm, with at least 4 perimeters locally, or the part bulges and cracks.
  • Press slowly and straight at the right temperature (about 200–230 °C for PLA/PETG; check the material), and stop when the insert is flush. Don't let it sink below the surface.
  • Orient the hole axis along Z (vertical) when you can; the hole is rounder, and the pull-out load runs across layers in the best way.

13.3 Tightening: preload and not crushing the plastic

A tightened screw stretches and clamps the parts together with a preload. Torque and preload are related by F = T / (K · d), where d is the thread diameter and K is about 0.2 for a dry steel screw. For example M3 at 0.4 N·m gives a preload near 670 N. That is more than a printed part can take: the head's contact area is small, and printed plastic crushes at roughly 20–40 MPa under a head.

  • Use a washer to spread the load; a wide washer can double or triple the bearing area.
  • Tighten to snug, not to the torque you'd use on metal. It is easy to exceed what plastic can bear with a small screwdriver.
  • Pass the clamp load through metal: put a brass or steel sleeve or spacer through the plastic, so the screw clamps the sleeve and the plastic merely sits around it. That makes a joint that doesn't crush or creep.
  • Creep loosens screws. Plastic under a steady compressive load flows slowly, and the preload falls. For anything that matters use a lock nut (nylon-insert), a spring or wave washer to keep the preload alive, or check and re-tighten after the first few days.
  • Thread-lockers and some chemicals can crack plastic: use them only on metal-to-metal threads and check compatibility.

13.4 Other joints

  • Dowels and alignment pins locate parts so screws only clamp. A pair of pins (or a pin and a slot, avoiding over-constraint, chapter 1) place two parts exactly. Press-fit 3 mm steel dowels into printed holes sized from a coupon (chapter 4).
  • Dovetails and T-slots slide together, carrying load across large faces; give 0.2–0.3 mm clearance and a stop.
  • Adhesives: cyanoacrylate (superglue) bonds PLA and PETG quickly but is brittle; epoxy fills gaps and is stronger; solvent welding (acetone) fuses ABS and ASA into one piece. The golden rules: large area, close fit (0.1–0.2 mm gap), clean surface, load in shear, never in peel. A scarf or step joint beats a butt joint.
  • Heat-staking / soldering-iron stitching: melt a pin head into a hole, or stitch a crack with a hot iron. Quick repair for plastics.
  • Joints and layers: a joint loaded so the layers peel apart is the weakest possible. Design the joint load to run across the layer plane in shear, or along the layers in tension (chapter 4).

13.5 Springs you buy

A steel spring is cheap, compact, doesn't creep, and has predictable force. For almost every job that needs a reliable force, buy it rather than print it (chapter 12).

TypeUseNotes
CompressionPushes: latches, buttons, plungers, preloadThe default. Needs a guide (rod or bore) to keep it from bowing.

Rubber bands, elastic cordCheap soft pullsAge in sunlight; fine for prototypes

For a round-wire helical compression spring of wire diameter d, mean coil diameter D and n active coils:

Rate k = G · d⁴ / (8 · D³ · n) (G ≈ 79 000 MPa for steel; k in N/mm)
Spring index C = D / d (good range 4–12)
Solid height ≈ (n + 2) · d (closed-and-ground ends)
Torsion spring: k = E · d⁴ / (64 · D · n) per radian of rotation (N·mm/rad)

Design habits: choose a spring that works at 30–70% of its travel, well away from solid height (fatigue: keep working deflection below ~75% of maximum), add preload so the spring isn't slack at assembly, and give it a guide with 0.5–1 mm clearance from the coil. For a free length more than about four times the coil diameter, an unguided spring will bow sideways.

13.7 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Fasteners chosen by reuse: inserts or nuts for anything opened more than twice.
  • ☐ Holes modelled with the printer's measured shrink; pockets for nuts and heads sized from the table.
  • ☐ Washers and snug torque; clamp load through a metal sleeve where it matters.
  • ☐ Insert holes and walls sized from the insert's datasheet.
  • ☐ Alignment by dowels or slots, not screws alone.
  • ☐ Glue joints designed for shear with a close fit.
  • ☐ A bought spring, guided, working at 30–70% travel.
  • Tightening a screw into plastic as hard as into steel.
  • Modelling a nut pocket at the exact nominal size, and then forcing the nut.
  • Using a printed coil spring as a permanent preload.
  • Cramming heat-set inserts into 1 mm walls.

13.8 Check yourself

An M3 screw is tightened to 0.4 N·m. What preload is that (K = 0.2), and why is it a problem in a printed part?

F = T/(K·d) = 0.4 / (0.2 × 0.003) ≈ 670 N. Under a 5.5 mm head over a 3.4 mm hole (area ≈ 15 mm²) that is ≈ 45 MPa: above what printed plastics usually take. Use a washer and a lower torque, or a sleeve.

A steel spring has d = 1 mm, D = 8 mm and 8 active coils. What is its rate?

k = G d⁴ / (8 D³ n) = 79000 × 1 / (8 × 512 × 8) = 79000 / 32768 ≈ 2.4 N/mm. Compressed 5 mm it pushes with about 12 N.

You need to open a lid fixed with four M3 screws every week. How do you fasten it?

Heat-set M3 inserts in the base (or captive nuts), through-clearance holes with counterbores in the lid, and a small washer under each head if the lid is thin. Direct threads in plastic would strip within a few cycles.
