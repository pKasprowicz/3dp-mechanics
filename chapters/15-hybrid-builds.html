Part C · Putting it together · Chapter 15

Hybrid builds: printed + off-the-shelf

The best printed mechanisms are not fully printed. They are a few printed parts that carry the shape, wrapped around a small set of bought parts that carry the precision and the load. This chapter is about drawing that line: what to print, what to buy, which standard sizes to design around, and how to make printed parts fit parts you didn't make.

15.1 The rule of thumb

A 3D printer is brilliant at custom shapes, one-offs, complicated geometry and quick iteration. It is poor at precision, hardness, wear resistance, stiffness and consistent material properties. Mass-produced standard parts are exactly the opposite: cheap, hard, precise, available in any size. So:

Print itBuy it
Housings, frames, brackets, mounts, coversShafts and rods (ground steel, precise diameter)
Links, levers, arms, cams (profile unique to you)Bearings (ball, linear, needle)
Knobs, handles, clips, cable guides, jigsScrews, nuts, washers, heat-set inserts, dowels
Light gears, pulleys for large pitches (HTD 5M), snap-fitsSprings, GT2 pulleys, belts, chains, cables
Complex ducts, enclosures, organic shapesMotors, servos, sensors, electronics, fans
Standoffs, spacers, alignment featuresLinear rails, V-wheels, extrusion, plates
Sleeves and housings around metal partsHigh-torque gears, keys, couplers, shaft collars
Magnet pockets, nut traps, bearing seatsMagnets, nuts, balls, rollers

A useful test for each part: Will its failure cost me more than a bought one would? A bracket you can reprint in an hour: print it. A bearing that must run smooth for years: buy it.

Deciding quickly: five questions

  1. Does it need exact dimensions or a smooth, hard surface? → buy (or finish: ream, sand, sleeve).
  2. Does it rub, roll or slide continuously? → bearing, bushing or a metal contact surface.
  3. Is it highly loaded or critical? → metal, or at least a metal insert, pin or sleeve.
  4. Is the shape unique to this project? → print.
  5. Does a standard version exist for a few euros? → buy; your time is worth more than the saving.

15.2 Standards to design around

Standardised sizes are a gift: every bolt pattern and diameter listed here is a fixed point your design can hang off. Always verify with calipers, since budget parts vary from the datasheet.

NEMA 17 is the universal printed-mechanism motor: body lengths of 34–48 mm (the length matters for torque, not for the mounting pattern), 1.8° per full step, and a 5 mm shaft, often with a flat. The pilot boss centres the motor on a hole: bore it to the pilot diameter + 0.3–0.5 mm. Screws go through the plate from outside into the motor's tapped holes: check the screw length so it doesn't bottom in the motor (typically no more than 3–4 mm of thread into a NEMA 17).

15.3 Designing around parts you bought


    
  
  • Buy first, design second. Get the motor, bearings, rods and magnets in hand and measure them with calipers before modelling the parts that hold them. Datasheet values for cheap parts are only nominal.
    • 
  
  • Add a reference model. Put simple solid models of the bought parts in your CAD assembly so you can see collisions, check screw lengths and verify the stack-up (a bearing 0.3 mm thicker than expected will jam a cover).
    • 
  
  • Parametric pockets. Make every seat a parameter (bearing_od = 22; fit = 0.0; shrink = 0.15), so adjusting for a new filament is a number change. Simulation 2 gives these.
    • 
  
  • Test the interface alone. Before a 6-hour print, print a 5-minute fit coupon containing only the pockets and holes: bearing seat, motor pilot, nut trap. It saves whole prints.
    • 
  
  • Make wear parts replaceable. Mount bearings, bushings and pads in a way that lets you swap them without re-printing.
    • 
  
  • Over-constraint again: where several bought parts must align (two rods, two bearings, a motor and a leadscrew) print the mounts in one piece, or give one a slot (chapters 1, 9, 10).

15.4 Interface patterns that work


Printed parts on a metal skeleton


Aluminium extrusion (2020) or a rigid plate carries the structure; printed parts are the brackets, mounts and moving pieces. The metal provides stiffness and alignment and the print provides the custom shapes. A printed bracket bolts to a T-slot with M5 screws and T-nuts; give the bolt hole 5.4–5.6 mm, add a pocket for the nut, and make the bracket 4–6 mm thick with ribs. Do not use printed parts as the main structure for large loads.


Reinforcing printed parts with metal


    
  
  • Steel or carbon-fibre rods through arms: embed a rod or tube in a long printed arm (drill or print a channel, glue it) to multiply the stiffness. An arm that bends under load becomes rigid.
    • 
  
  • Brass or steel sleeves through bolt holes carry the clamp load (chapter 13).
    • 
  
  • Metal pins in hinges and latches; steel inserts in threaded holes.
    • 
  
  • Plywood, acrylic or aluminium plates as bases, with printed standoffs and feet.
    • 



Shafts and hubs


Steel shaft, bearings in the housing, and a printed hub fixed to the shaft with a D-flat and grub screw: the standard hybrid rotary axis (chapter 10).


Electronics


    
  
  • Mount boards on standoffs (printed or brass) with the right screw size (M2.5 for Pi-type boards, M3 for many others) and allow for connector access and cable bend radius.
    • 
  
  • Strain relief for every cable that moves or can be tugged: a clamp, a tie-down point, a loop.
    • 
  
  • Motors and drivers get hot. Leave ventilation or a heatsink; keep PLA away from heat sources (chapter 3).
    • 
  
  • Keep wiring channels in the model: a part that "has room for the wires" on paper usually doesn't.
    • 




15.5 Sourcing and a first shopping list


Buy in assortments first, then replace with exact sizes once you know what you use. Prefer reputable suppliers for bearings, linear rails and motors; cheap ones are fine for screws, nuts and magnets.


    
  
  • ☐ Digital calipers (the most important tool you will buy).
    • 
  
  • ☐ M2, M2.5, M3, M4 socket-head screw assortment (6–40 mm), nuts, washers, and thread-forming screws.
    • 
  
  • ☐ Heat-set inserts (M2, M3, M4) and a soldering iron with insert tips.
    • 
  
  • ☐ Bearings: 623, 624, 625, 608 (and 688); a few LM8UU if you plan linear axes.
    • 
  
  • ☐ Steel rods: 3, 4, 5, 6, 8 mm, 300–500 mm lengths; a hacksaw and file.
    • 
  
  • ☐ Springs: a compression-spring assortment; extension and torsion for specific jobs.
    • 
  
  • ☐ Motion: a NEMA 17 + driver (A4988/TMC2209-class), a few hobby servos, GT2 belt and 16/20T pulleys, a T8 leadscrew + nut, flexible couplers (5→8 mm).
    • 
  
  • ☐ Structure: a few lengths of 2020 extrusion, T-nuts, M5 bolts, corner brackets.
    • 
  
  • ☐ Misc: disc magnets (6×3, 8×3, 10×3), steel balls (6 mm), D-shaft keys or grub screws, silicone and PTFE grease, cyanoacrylate + epoxy, zip ties, heat-shrink, hex keys, small drill bits (3, 4, 5, 8 mm) for reaming.

15.8 Check yourself


You need to mount a NEMA 17 on a printed plate. Where do the four screw holes go and how big are the holes?

A 31 mm square pattern: holes at (±15.5, ±15.5) mm from the shaft axis, M3 clearance (3.4 mm nominal, plus your shrink). The pilot opening at the centre is Ø22 mm + 0.3–0.5 mm clearance + shrink. Cut the screw holes as slots if the motor must move to tension a belt.


You measure a 608 bearing at 22.02 mm OD and your printer shrinks holes 0.18 mm. What do you model for a snug fit (−0.02)?

22.02 − 0.02 + 0.18 = 22.18 mm. Check it on a fit coupon; add a shoulder and cover so the bearing is captured regardless.


A printed gear costs €0.40 in filament and 40 minutes of machine time. A bought equivalent costs €2. Which should you use for a one-off prototype and which for a production part?

Prototype: print it. It is cheaper and shaped exactly as needed; the test tells you whether it works. Production or load-bearing: consider buying: the €2 part is harder, more consistent and will not wear out or creep.
