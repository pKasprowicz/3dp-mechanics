Mechanics you need

You do not need a mechanical-engineering degree to design printed mechanisms, but you do need about ten ideas and the habit of putting numbers on them. This chapter is those ten ideas, with calculators you can play with, aimed at the questions you will actually ask: Is this motor strong enough? Will this arm bend? Why did it crack at that corner?

About the numbers Material values here are typical, order-of-magnitude figures for FDM parts, used to build intuition and for a first sizing. Real strength depends on your printer, settings, filament brand, moisture, and orientation. Treat results as "will it survive, roughly", then confirm with a printed test. Do not use these for safety-critical parts (see 2.9).

2.1 Force, moment, torque

Force pushes or pulls: unit newton (N). 1 kg hanging from a string pulls with about 9.81 N. Moment (or torque) is a force's turning effect about a point: M = F × d, where d is the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of the force. Unit: N·m (or N·mm).

Two habits will take you far:

  1. Draw a free-body diagram. Isolate one part, draw it as a blob, and draw every force and moment acting on it, one arrow per contact or load. Most "mystery" failures turn out to be a force you forgot to draw.
  2. Static equilibrium: if the part is not accelerating, forces sum to zero and moments about any point sum to zero. Pick the point that kills the most unknowns (usually a pivot).
Units trap Hobby motor and servo torque is quoted in kgf·cm or oz·in. Convert: 1 kgf·cm = 0.0981 N·m, 1 oz·in = 0.00706 N·m. A "20 kg·cm" servo can hold a 20 kg load on a 1 cm arm, or 2 kg on a 10 cm arm. Force at the end of an arm is always F = T / r.

2.2 Levers, mechanical advantage, work and power

A lever balances moments about its fulcrum, so a small force on a long arm balances a big force on a short arm: F_effort × b = F_load × a. The ratio b/a is the mechanical advantage. But nothing is free: the effort end moves b/a times farther. This is conservation of work (ideal case: force × distance in = force × distance out).

The rotary version of the same trade is power: P = T × ω (W = N·m × rad/s). For a given motor power, gearing down trades speed for torque and gearing up does the reverse. Every gear train, pulley or leadscrew you meet later is this idea in costume. Real systems lose some power to friction, so efficiency is below 100%: typically 90%+ for spur gears, 30–50% for steep worm gears.

2.3 Friction

Coulomb friction is the usual model: friction force is proportional to the normal force, F_f ≤ μ·N, and acts opposite to (impending) sliding. There are two coefficients: static μₛ (to start sliding) is higher than kinetic μₖ (once sliding). That gap is what causes stick-slip: the squeal of a door hinge or the jerky motion of a slowly pushed drawer.

Pair (dry, rough guide)μ approx.
Printed PLA / PETG on itself (rough layered surface)0.3 – 0.6, can be higher
Nylon on steel0.2 – 0.4
PTFE-based on steel0.05 – 0.15
Plastic on plastic with thin silicone/PTFE lubricant0.05 – 0.15
Rubber / TPU on a clean surface0.6 – 1.5
Rolling (bearing) equivalent0.001 – 0.01

Two practical consequences:

  • The friction angle. A surface at slope θ holds a block still while tan θ ≤ μ, i.e. θ ≤ arctan μ. The same rule decides whether a screw self-locks (a lead angle smaller than the friction angle means the load cannot back-drive it), whether a wedge holds, and whether a cam follower binds.
  • Friction does not depend on contact area in this simple model, but wear and heat do. A bigger printed bearing surface does not slide easier; it just wears slower.
3D printing Printed surfaces are rough, with ridges every layer height, and the ridges lock into each other. Printed-on-printed sliding therefore has a higher μ than the textbook value and wears fast (PLA on PLA gets "sticky"). Remedies you will see across the chapters: sliding on a metal rod or smooth bushing instead, using a printed-in-nylon or PTFE-filled insert, orienting the sliding face parallel to the bed (clean top layers), adding a thin lubricant, and making sliding parts from different materials.

2.4 Stress, strain, stiffness and strength

A force on its own tells you little. What damages a part is force per unit area:

Normal stress σ = F / A (tension or compression, N/mm² = MPa)
Shear stress τ = F / A (force parallel to the area)
Strain ε = ΔL / L (relative stretch, dimensionless)
Hooke's law σ = E · ε (E = Young's modulus, the material's stiffness)

Two very different material numbers, and they're often confused:

  • Strength (yield or ultimate stress): how much stress before it permanently deforms or breaks.
  • Stiffness (E): how much it stretches under a given stress. A material can be strong and floppy (TPU, nylon) or stiff and brittle (PLA).

Brittle materials (PLA, many CF-filled filaments) give little warning: they crack. Ductile ones (PETG, nylon, ASA, ABS) yield and bend first. For anything that must not suddenly fail, prefer a ductile material, or add enough margin that you stay far below the break.

Typical FDM, ballparkE (GPa)Tensile strength, along layers (MPa)Behaviour

Chapter 3 goes into materials in depth, including your printer's specifics. For now, keep the shape: plastics are 20–100× less stiff than steel (E ≈ 200 GPa) and 5–10× weaker, so printed parts are bulky and deflect more.

2.5 Beams: bending is what usually kills your part

Most printed parts fail in bending, not pure tension: arms, brackets, levers, tabs, spokes, anything that is a beam. Bending puts one side in tension and the other in compression, with the maximum at the surface farthest from the centre. For a rectangle (width b, thickness h in the bending direction):

Second moment of area I = b·h³ / 12
Bending stress σ = M·c / I → cantilever end load: σ = 6·F·L / (b·h²)
Cantilever deflection δ = F·L³ / (3·E·I)
Simply supported, centre load: δ = F·L³ / (48·E·I)

Read these like a designer, not a mathematician:

  • Thickness is cubed in stiffness (h³) and squared in strength (h²). Doubling the thickness makes it 8× stiffer and 4× less stressed. Doubling the width only doubles both. Make the part deeper in the direction of bending; ribs and flanges exist for this reason.
  • Length is cubed in deflection (L³). A 30% longer arm deflects more than twice as much. Shorten the overhang before you thicken it.
  • Hollow is almost as good as solid in bending, since the material near the centre carries little stress. This is why tubes, I-beams and 3-perimeter walls with sparse infill work.

Shear in pins and bolts. A pin loaded across its axis is in shear: τ = F / A per shear plane (single shear: one plane, double shear: two planes, twice the capacity). A steel M3 screw is very rarely the weak link. The plastic around the hole usually is, through bearing stress σ_b = F / (d × t) (hole diameter × plate thickness). It crushes, ovalizes and creeps, so keep it low.

Buckling. A long slender part in compression fails by suddenly bowing sideways, well before its material strength. Euler's load is P_cr = π²·E·I / (K·L)² (K = 1 for pinned ends, 2 for one end free, 0.5 for fixed). Same lesson: it scales with I (depth) and falls with L². Slender printed push-rods and linkage members are prime buckling candidates.

2.6 Torsion (twisting)

A shaft transmitting torque T has shear stress τ = 16·T / (π·d³) for a solid round shaft of diameter d: cubic in diameter, so a 20% larger shaft is 1.7× stronger. Printed shafts have a second problem: layer lines. Torque transmitted across layer interfaces is weak, and the connection from shaft to printed hub (keys, set-screw flats, D-profiles) concentrates stress. As a rule, do not transmit significant torque through a thin printed shaft; use a steel or brass rod and let the plastic be a hub or housing (chapters 10 and 15).

2.7 Stress concentrations: why parts crack at corners

The stress formulas above give nominal stress for smooth shapes. Wherever a section changes abruptly (a sharp inside corner, a hole, a groove, the root of a thread), the stress flow bunches up and the local peak stress is σ_peak = Kt × σ_nominal. For a sharp internal corner Kt can be 3 to 10+. A simple estimate for a notch of depth d and root radius r is Kt ≈ 1 + 2·√(d / r) (pessimistic for gentle fillets). The practical lesson is the cheapest in the whole book: put a fillet on every inside corner.

3D printing Printing makes it worse: the layer boundary at a sharp corner is a built-in crack starter, and brittle materials like PLA feel the full Kt. A 1–2 mm fillet at the root of a snap-fit hook, a lever, or a screw boss often changes a part from "snaps at first use" to "lasts years". It also does not cost anything in CAD.

2.8 What is special about printed plastics

  • Anisotropy. Parts are strong along the extruded lines and weak between layers (typically 30–70% of the in-layer strength, lower for brittle or poorly-bonded prints). Orient parts so the main tensile or bending stress runs along layers, never pulling them apart. Chapter 4 makes this a design rule.
  • Creep and stress relaxation. Under constant load, plastics slowly deform further; under constant deformation, the force relaxes. A PLA clip that's tight on day one is loose after weeks. For parts under permanent load, derate strength drastically (a factor 0.3–0.5 for PLA) or pick a more creep-resistant material (PETG, ASA, CF-nylon), or let a metal part carry the sustained load.
  • Temperature. Stiffness and strength fall as plastic warms, long before it melts. PLA gets soft around 55–60 °C, which a car dashboard in the sun reaches. Heated motors and hot-ends are close neighbours.
  • Fatigue. Repeated loading breaks things at stresses far below their static strength. A snap-fit or living hinge flexed 10,000 times needs strain well below the one-shot limit.
  • Moisture. Nylon (and to a lesser extent PETG/PLA) absorbs water, which changes stiffness and dimensions.
  • Infill, perimeters. For bending and torsion, the outer walls carry the load. Adding perimeters (and top/bottom layers) strengthens a part far more per gram than adding infill.

2.9 Safety factors

The safety factor is SF = allowable stress / actual stress. The allowable is the material strength after derating for orientation, creep, temperature and stress concentration. Then you add a margin for the unknown. A reasonable starting point for printed parts:

SituationMinimum SF on derated strength
Static, well-known load, forgiving failure (a desk gadget)2
Normal use, loads somewhat uncertain3 – 4
Repeated loading, impact, sustained load5 – 10 (or test)
Failure could injure someone or cause damageDo not rely on a printed part alone; use a metal part and, if possible, a design that fails safe

For critical parts such as climbing, lifting, vehicle, medical or pressure-bearing, use certified components. Printing is not the right way to make them.

2.10 A quick-estimate workflow

  1. Write down the loads in newtons (weights, motor torque ÷ radius, spring forces, a person pushing: 20–100 N is typical; a hard push or drop can be several times that).
  2. Sketch a free-body diagram of the part; find the reaction forces and the worst moment.
  3. Reduce the part to a beam, pin, or shaft and compute stress with the formulas above.
  4. Take material strength from the table, derate for orientation (×0.5 across layers), creep (×0.4 if sustained) and stress concentrations (÷Kt, or fillet them).
  5. Check deflection too, since it is often what really limits a mechanism: a stiff-enough arm that still flexes 3 mm will wreck gear mesh or sensor alignment.
  6. Print a test part, load it, and measure. A bathroom scale and some stacked weights is a legitimate test rig.

2.11 Common mistakes

  • Designing in tension when the part is actually in bending: bending stresses are far higher than F/A suggests.
  • Making a long, thin arm and then being surprised by the L³ deflection.
  • Sharp inside corners on any highly stressed part.
  • Printing a part so that the load pulls layers apart (Z-direction tension): the weakest direction.
  • Sizing a motor for holding force but ignoring peak accelerations and friction (see ch. 16).
  • Forgetting units: kg vs N, mm vs m, kgf·cm vs N·m.

2.12 Check yourself

A servo is rated 5 kgf·cm. What force can it exert at the end of a 25 mm horn?

T = 5 × 0.0981 = 0.49 N·m. F = T / r = 0.49 / 0.025 ≈ 19.6 N (about 2 kg). In practice use half or less of the rated stall torque.

An arm deflects 4 mm and carries stress near its limit. You make it 1.5× thicker (deeper). What happens?

Stiffness ∝ h³: 1.5³ = 3.4× stiffer, so the deflection drops to about 1.2 mm. Stress ∝ 1/h²: it drops to 1/2.25 ≈ 44%. Well worth the extra plastic.

A tab breaks at a sharp inside corner. The part is PLA, the load is modest. What do you try first?

Add a 1–2 mm fillet at that corner (Kt falls sharply), check that the layers run along the tab, and only then think about material or thickness.

A block on a plastic ramp starts to slide at 25°. What is μₛ?

μₛ = tan 25° ≈ 0.47.
