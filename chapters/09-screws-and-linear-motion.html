Screws and linear motion

Two things make a straight-line mechanism work: something that pushes (a screw, a belt, a rack) and something that guides (a rod, a rail, a slot). Most failed printed sliders fail at the guide, not at the drive: they bind, they wobble, or they jam when pushed off-centre. This chapter covers both halves, with the one rule that prevents most sliding jams.

9.1 Threads and power screws

A screw converts rotation to linear motion because a nut on a thread must advance when it turns. Four numbers describe a thread:

TermMeaning
Pitch, PDistance between adjacent thread crests.
Starts, nNumber of independent threads wound in parallel (1, 2, 4...).
Lead, lDistance the nut moves per turn: l = n · P. This is what sets the speed.
Lead angle, λλ = atan( l / (π · d_m) ) where dm is the mean (pitch) diameter. Steeper λ means faster, less friction-dominated, and not self-locking.

A common hobby leadscrew, T8×8, is a 8 mm trapezoidal screw with lead 8 mm: four starts of 2 mm pitch. The same size with T8×2 has a single start and lead 2 mm: four times slower, four times more force from the same torque, and it holds position by itself.

Force, efficiency and self-locking

Friction in the thread decides everything. With friction coefficient μ on a flank of half-angle β, define an effective μ′ = μ / cos β. Then:

Forward efficiency η = tan λ · (1 − μ′·tan λ) / (tan λ + μ′)
Axial force from torque F = 2π · η · T / l
Self-locking when tan λ ≤ μ′ (the load cannot turn the screw back)
Speed v = l · rpm / 60

Self-locking and high efficiency are opposites: a screw that holds its load by itself (shallow λ) spends most of the motor's torque fighting friction (η typically 20–35%), while a steep screw is efficient (60–80%) but can be back-driven by the load. For a vertical axis, either use a self-locking screw, or a motor brake, or a counterweight.

Backlash and anti-backlash nuts

A nut needs clearance to turn on the screw. That clearance is backlash: the axial free play when you reverse. Printers and CNCs fix it by preloading the nut so that one flank is always in contact:

  • Spring-loaded split nut: two half-nuts pushed apart by a spring.
  • Two nuts with a spring or a spacer between them.
  • Flexible/compliant nut: a printed nut with a slit and a screw-tightened clamp, or flexures that squeeze the thread.
  • Vertical axes use gravity as the preload: no anti-backlash needed.

Mounting a leadscrew

  • The screw drives; the guides guide. If the screw also tries to guide the carriage, the two fight (over-constraint, chapter 1) and you get binding and "Z wobble" ripples. Let the nut float in its carrier (a slot or a loose-fitting mount that allows sideways movement but takes the axial load), and keep the screw straight and aligned to the rails.
  • Support the end with a thrust-capable bearing. The motor shaft bearings are not designed to carry the full axial load of a screw. A small angular-contact or a flat thrust bearing at the free end is cheap.
  • Use a flexible coupler (helical, bellows or Oldham) between the motor and the screw to absorb shaft misalignment. A rigid coupler wobbles the screw.
  • Critical speed: a long thin screw whips like a skipping rope at high rpm. Simulation 3 calculates the limit.

Printed threads

  • Where they make sense: screw caps, jar lids, adjusters, clamps, vices, light-duty jacks, hand-turned knobs, and levelling feet. Not high-speed drives.
  • Print vertically (axis along Z) so the thread is round and the layers wrap around the screw. A horizontal thread prints oval and fuses badly.
  • Use coarse pitch (2–4 mm) and a square or trapezoidal form; fine ISO threads below 1.5 mm pitch are mostly noise at 0.4 mm nozzle resolution.
  • Give 0.2–0.4 mm of radial clearance between mating threads, chamfer the entry, and test with a coupon (chapter 4).
  • Multi-start threads (2–4 starts) give quick opening and closing with the same pitch, like a jar lid.
  • For anything that matters, buy the screw (a T8 leadscrew is a few euros) and print only the housing. A printed nut on a steel screw works for light duty if it is nylon or lubricated; brass or POM nuts last far longer.
  • For bolted joints, use metal fasteners with heat-set inserts or captive nuts (chapter 13). Don't rely on a printed internal thread for anything that will be tightened repeatedly.

9.2 Guiding linear motion

A sliding carriage must be allowed one degree of freedom (along the track) and have the other five removed. Choose a guide by load, precision and cost:

GuideStrengthWeaknessPrinting notes
Printed rod-in-bore or dovetail/T-slotFree, no partsFriction, play, wear, stick-slipOK for light, slow, rarely used parts. Nylon + a little lubricant; one printed part sliding in another is a classic jam.

The rule that prevents jamming

A carriage pushed with a force F applied at distance a from the guide's axis tries to tilt. It is held by two contact points at the ends of its guide span L, so the normal force at each is N = F·a/L. Friction at both contacts is 2μN. The carriage jams (the friction exceeds the push) when

2 · μ · F · a / L ≥ F → a ≥ L / (2μ)

In words: the span must be longer than 2μ times the push offset (L > 2μ·a). With μ = 0.3 the span must exceed 0.6× the offset; with a sticky μ = 0.6 it must exceed 1.2×. The cure is always the same: push close to the guide axis, make the guide span long, or reduce μ. This is why a drawer pushed at one corner binds while the same drawer pushed in the middle glides, why a short printed bushing on a long arm jams, and why leadscrew nuts should push centrally.

Over-constraint in linear guides

Remember chapter 1: two parallel rods each fix the carriage's tilt and position. If they're not exactly parallel (and printed frames never are), the carriage binds. Cures:

  • Use one precise guide (a rod/rail) plus a second, loose or compliant support (a slot, a free-floating bearing, a flat surface).
  • Print both rod mounts in one part, so the parallelism comes from the print, not from separate brackets.
  • If using two rods with ball bearings, mount one bearing on a flexure or a slightly loose holder.
  • Check by hand: the carriage should move by a fingertip with no power and no "tight spots" along the stroke before you add the drive.

9.3 Critical speed and long screws

A long, slender screw has a natural bending frequency, and spinning near it makes it whip violently. For a steel screw with root diameter d and unsupported length L (both in mm):

n_critical ≈ 1.22 × 10⁸ · k · d / L² [rpm]
k = 0.36 (fixed–free), 1.0 (pinned–pinned), 1.56 (fixed–pinned), 2.27 (fixed–fixed)
Run at ≤ 80% of this value.

Fixes for a screw that whips: shorten the unsupported length, support the free end with a bearing, use a larger diameter, or slow it down. A leadscrew with a bent shaft wobbles at every speed, so check straightness by rolling it on a flat surface.

9.4 Vertical axes and holding loads

  • A belt or a high-lead (non-self-locking) screw lets the load fall when the motor loses power. A stepper motor's holding torque disappears with the power.
  • Options: a self-locking low-lead screw; a brake on the motor; a counterweight or gas/constant-force spring that cancels most of the weight; a worm drive (chapter 7).
  • For a Z axis carrying a heavy printed head, a counterweight or spring both saves motor torque and avoids crashing on power loss.

9.6 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Screw: lead chosen for speed and force (use Simulation 1); locking vs efficiency decided on purpose.
  • ☐ Thrust is carried by a proper axial bearing, not the motor shaft.
  • ☐ Nut floats; the guides do the guiding.
  • ☐ Guide span L > 2μ·a; the load is pushed close to the guide axis.
  • ☐ Not two rigid, separately aligned parallel guides.
  • ☐ Critical speed checked for long screws; vertical axis has a brake or counterweight.
  • ☐ Free-motion check by hand before the motor goes on.
  • Printing a long thread horizontally and wondering why it's oval and tight.
  • Using a rigid coupler on a screw that is not perfectly aligned with the motor.
  • Mounting a single short bushing at the end of a long arm and pushing at the tip.
  • Choosing a leadscrew for speed (high lead) and then wanting it to hold position without power.

9.7 Check yourself

A T8×2 screw (lead 2 mm) and a T8×8 (lead 8 mm) use the same 0.3 N·m motor. Compare their ideal thrust (ignoring friction).

F = 2π·T/l. For lead 2 mm: 2π·0.3/0.002 ≈ 940 N. For lead 8 mm: ≈ 235 N. Four times less thrust but four times the speed. (Real thrust is further reduced by efficiency: much more so for the low-lead screw.)

A carriage with a 30 mm guide span is pushed from 25 mm off the rod's axis. With μ = 0.3, does it jam?

Jam if a ≥ L/(2μ) = 30 / 0.6 = 50 mm. Here a = 25 mm < 50 mm, so it slides (but with high friction: at 25 mm you are halfway to the limit). Halve the span or double the offset and it jams.

Why can't you simply tighten two parallel rods and bearings to remove the play?

Because two rigid guides fix the same degrees of freedom twice. Any slight non-parallelism makes the carriage bind. Use one precise guide and let the other float or flex.
