Rotary joints, bearings and shafts

Every linkage pivot, every gear shaft, every wheel and every turntable is a rotary joint. This is the single most worthwhile place to spend money on bought parts: a few bearings and some steel rod turn a rattly printed assembly into something that feels like a machine. This chapter shows when a printed bearing is enough, how to choose and mount a real one, and how to connect shafts to everything else.

10.1 What a rotary joint has to do

A rotating joint allows one rotation and removes everything else. It has to carry radial load (sideways, across the shaft), axial / thrust load (along the shaft) and sometimes a tilting moment, with low friction and little play. Pick the simplest joint that handles the real loads:

JointGood forLimits
Pin or screw in a printed holeHinges, linkages, light and occasional rotationWears and gets sloppy fast; stick-slip
Plain bushing (printed, polymer or bronze sleeve)Slow-to-medium speeds, moderate loads, quiet, cheapPV limit (heat/wear); needs lubricant or the right material pair
Ball bearing (deep-groove)Speed, low friction, precision, long lifeCost; needs a proper seat; not for shocks
Thrust bearing / thrust washerAxial loads (turntables, leadscrews)Ball radial bearings carry little axial load
Needle / rollerHigh load in a small diameterNeed hardened shaft or races
Flexure pivotTiny rotation, no friction, no playLimited angle (chapter 12)

10.2 Plain bearings: the PV rule

A plain bearing is a shaft sliding in a sleeve. It fails from heat and wear, and the limit is captured by the PV value: the bearing pressure times the sliding speed.

Bearing pressure P = F / (d · L) (projected area, MPa when F in N and d, L in mm)
Sliding speed v = π · d · n / 60000 (m/s, d in mm, n in rpm)
PV = P · v (MPa·m/s) friction heat ≈ μ · F · v (W)

Every bearing material has a PV limit above which it overheats, softens and wears quickly. Printed plastics on a steel shaft have low limits; polymer-bearing materials and sintered bronze have far higher ones. Lubrication raises the limit several-fold and also reduces the friction.

Practical conclusions:

  • Slow and light (a hinge, a knob, a pivot turning a few times a minute): a printed hole with a steel pin is fine. Use PETG or nylon, not PLA.
  • Moderate (a wheel on an axle, a roller, a pulley, rpm in the tens to hundreds): press a brass, bronze or polymer sleeve into the printed hole. Cheap, and PV limits go up by 10×.
  • Fast or heavy or precise (a motor-driven shaft, a gear train): ball bearings.
  • Length: bearing length ≈ 1–1.5× the shaft diameter. Very long bushings are sensitive to misalignment (ends carry all the load).

10.3 Ball bearings: choosing and seating them

A deep-groove ball bearing is the cheapest, most useful precision part you can buy. It has an inner ring (fits on the shaft), an outer ring (fits in the housing), and balls between them. Common sizes (bore × outer diameter × width, mm):

BearingBore × OD × widthTypical use
6833 × 7 × 3Tiny idlers, small robots
6933 × 8 × 4Small pulleys, idlers
6233 × 10 × 4The workhorse for M3 shafts and idler pulleys
6244 × 13 × 5M4 shafts, belt idlers
6255 × 16 × 5M5 shafts

6888 × 16 × 5Compact 8 mm shafts, wheels

Suffixes: ZZ metal shields (light protection, spins freely), 2RS rubber seals (better dirt protection, a little more drag). Skip unneeded drag in light mechanisms with ZZ.

Seating a bearing in a printed housing

  • Design the bore from your measured hole shrink (chapter 4's coupon). The printed bore ends up smaller than modelled; model it larger by the shrink amount.
  • Fit type: a light press (0.05–0.1 mm interference) holds the bearing without anything else, but a printed housing creeps and relaxes over weeks. Do not rely on friction alone for anything important: add a shoulder on one side and a cover plate or retaining ring on the other, so the bearing is captured mechanically.
  • Don't squeeze too hard. A bearing in a too-tight bore deforms its outer ring, gets stiff and gritty and wears out. If it won't turn freely by hand after pressing, the bore is too small.
  • Wall thickness: at least 3 mm, or 20% of the bearing OD, whichever is larger; thin rings split or ovalise.
  • The shoulder lip should land on the outer ring only: about 1–1.5 mm of overlap, never touching the inner ring or the seal.
  • Press bearings in square, using a socket or washer against the outer ring (never press on the inner ring or balls); a vice with two flat blocks works well. A little heat (hair dryer) on the housing helps with printed parts.

On the shaft

  • The inner ring must be clamped axially against a shoulder or collar by a nut, a screw, an E-clip, or the next part; otherwise it creeps and spins.
  • A screw or bolt as a shaft is the cheapest option (M3 for 623, M5 for 625, M8 for 608). A bolt's thread is a little under nominal size, so a shoulder bolt (with a true unthreaded shoulder) gives a proper fit.
  • Use two bearings spaced apart for any shaft that carries a load or an overhang; one bearing alone cannot resist tilting.

10.4 Shafts and connecting them to things

A shaft is a rod. The standard choice is ground steel rod (silver steel, precision shafts in 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10 mm): round, hard, smooth, and far stronger than anything printed. A bolt or threaded rod is the cheap alternative where precision matters less. Printed shafts only work for tiny loads or slow hand-turned mechanisms.

Torque must pass between the shaft and each part on it (gear, pulley, cam, hub). Options:

MethodCapacityNotes
Round hole + grub (set) screw on the round shaftLowDigs a divot and loosens; fine for light loads only
D-shaft (flat) + grub screw on the flatMedium–highThe cheap, strong, standard answer; use a nut trap for the grub screw in the printed hub
Split clamp hub with a screwMediumWorks well in print; clamping force relaxes if the plastic creeps
Keyway + keyHighHard to print neatly; for metal hubs
Cross pin / roll pinHighDrill through hub and shaft; reliable
Hex or square shaft + matching holeHighPrints well; hex holes need a small clearance
Metal hub (bought) in the printed partHighBest for gears under real load
Press fit into the printed holeLow–mediumCreeps and loosens; avoid for torque

Axial retention of shafts: E-clips or circlips in a groove (a groove in a steel rod is cut or bought), shaft collars with a grub screw, a nut and washer on a bolt, or a printed shoulder plus a cover.

10.5 Couplings

A coupling joins two shafts end to end (motor to leadscrew, motor to gearbox). The shafts are never perfectly aligned, so choose by what it must absorb:

CouplingAbsorbsNotes
Rigid (sleeve with grub screws, clamp)NothingOnly if the shafts are held in alignment by one rigid part; otherwise it bends the shafts and wrecks bearings
Helical / beam (a cut spiral tube)Angular + parallel misalignment; torsionally stiffThe standard bought coupling for steppers and leadscrews
Jaw / spider (elastomer insert)All three; damps vibrationPrintable hubs with a TPU spider
Oldham (two hubs + a slotted disc)Parallel offsetPrints well in 3 parts; sliding faces need lubricant
Universal (cardan) jointLarge angular misalignmentSpeed fluctuates unless used in pairs
Flexible shaft / tubingEverything, little torqueToy-level

Misalignment types: parallel (shifted axes), angular (tilted), and axial (the ends move together/apart). Rule: if two shafts are each held by their own bearings, the coupling between them must be flexible. That is the over-constraint rule of chapter 1 again.

10.6 Hinges and pivots

  • Pin hinge: two knuckles and a pin (a screw, a steel pin, filament). Use a pin through a clevis (fork) so the pin is loaded in double shear. The hole in each knuckle should be a snug fit on the pin and a looser fit in the fixed part.
  • Print-in-place hinge (chapter 4): needs 0.3–0.5 mm clearance and a break-free step; only light duty.
  • Living hinge (a thin flexing strip): chapter 12.
  • Friction/detent hinge: a washer, a spring or a printed ratchet holds the angle.
  • Axial stops: washers keep links from rubbing flat faces and leave a defined side gap.

10.7 Thrust loads and turntables

Radial ball bearings tolerate a little axial load; heavy axial load (a turntable under a weight, a leadscrew) needs a thrust bearing (flat race) or, cheaply, a thrust washer (PTFE, nylon or bronze) between a shoulder and the rotating part. A printed lazy-susan is a ring of 6–8 mm steel balls in two printed grooved rings (groove radius a little larger than the ball radius, smooth surfaces, a printed cage to space the balls): inexpensive, big diameter, surprisingly smooth. A wheel-on-a-ring alternative (3–4 skate bearings on a printed track) is even easier.

10.8 Materials, lubrication and care

  • Shaft: steel, polished. Not PLA on PLA.
  • Sleeve / hole: nylon or PETG against steel; avoid printing a bearing surface on a vertical wall with visible layer ridges if you can ream it.
  • Lubricant: silicone or PTFE grease for plastic-on-steel. Mineral oils and some greases can cause stress cracking in ABS, polycarbonate and some other plastics: check compatibility. Don't lubricate a press fit you're relying on.
  • Ball bearings: fine in dusty places if sealed. Keep chips and printing debris out of the housing; do not wash bearings with solvents that dissolve their grease.
  • Reaming: run a drill bit of exact size by hand through a printed hole to smooth it, remove stringing and round it up.

10.10 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Joint type chosen from the real loads (PV, thrust, tilt).
  • ☐ Shaft is steel (or a shoulder bolt); torque transferred via a flat, pin or hex, not just friction.
  • ☐ Bearing bore modelled from measured shrink; shoulder on one side, retainer on the other.
  • ☐ Inner ring clamped axially; outer ring seat squeezes nothing.
  • ☐ Two bearings, span ≥ 2× overhang.
  • ☐ Flexible coupling between separately supported shafts.
  • ☐ Thrust path planned.
  • Press-fitting a bearing in a thin printed ring that cracks at the first tightening.
  • Using a PLA hole as a bearing for a motor-driven shaft.
  • Using one bearing on a long overhanging shaft.
  • Rigidly coupling a motor to a leadscrew held by its own bearing.

10.11 Check yourself

A 6 mm steel shaft turns at 300 rpm in a 10 mm long printed bushing under a 20 N radial load. What are P, v and PV?

P = 20/(6 × 10) = 0.33 MPa. v = π × 6 × 300/60000 = 0.094 m/s. PV = 0.031 MPa·m/s. That's right at the limit for a dry printed bearing: use a bronze/polymer sleeve, lubricant, or a ball bearing for continuous running.

A shaft has two bearings 40 mm apart with 0.1 mm of total radial play, and a pulley 30 mm beyond the second bearing. What is the rattle at the pulley?

Peak-to-peak ≈ c·(1 + 2a/S) = 0.1 × (1 + 2×30/40) = 0.1 × 2.5 = 0.25 mm. Space the bearings 60 mm apart and it drops to 0.2 mm; move the pulley closer and it falls further.

Why do two bearings on one shaft sometimes bind even though each is fine alone?

Their seats are not perfectly coaxial, so the shaft is held at two slightly different centres: over-constraint. Print both seats in one part, ream them together, or let one bearing float.
