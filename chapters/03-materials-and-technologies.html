Materials and printing technologies

For a mechanism the material is not a colour choice. It decides whether a hinge lasts a thousand cycles or a million, whether a gear whines, and whether a part still holds its shape in a warm car. This chapter maps the technologies, the materials that matter for moving parts, and what your Bambu Lab X2D can and cannot print.

FDM is where this book lives, because you own one and it is the most flexible. Keep SLS in mind as an order-it-out option for the one part that will not behave in FDM (a high-load gear, a complex enclosed mechanism).

3.2 Your printer: Bambu Lab X2D + AMS 2 Pro

Verify before you rely on this The figures below come from retailer listings and community discussions (sources at the bottom). I could not reach the official Bambu Lab specification page, and the sources disagree in places. Check the Bambu Lab Wiki and your own machine's material profiles before choosing a material for something important.
  • Enclosed CoreXY with active chamber heating, reported up to about 65 °C, and a bed up to about 120 °C. A warm chamber helps warp-prone materials (ABS, ASA, nylon, PC) and generally improves layer adhesion, meaning stronger parts.
  • Maximum nozzle temperature reported as 300 °C. That covers PLA, PETG, ABS/ASA, most nylons and many carbon-filled grades. It is a limit for the highest-temperature materials (PPS-CF, some high-temperature PA-CF, PEEK/PEI-class), which need a 350 °C nozzle. Those are out of scope for this book anyway.
  • Dual-nozzle system ("D"): the second nozzle can run a different material without constant purging, such as a soluble or breakaway support, or a second structural material. Practical reading: print a tough part with an interface layer that releases cleanly, which is very useful for print-in-place joints. Your build volume is reported around 256 × 256 × 260 mm on the main nozzle and somewhat less on the second; check the wiki.
  • AMS 2 Pro feeds four spools and has built-in drying (reported around 65 °C). Good for PLA, PETG, TPU and most others. Nylon and some PC ideally want a hotter, longer drying cycle, so keep a dedicated filament dryer around for those.
  • Abrasive filaments (carbon fibre, glass fibre) wear brass nozzles quickly. Use a hardened-steel (or equivalent) nozzle for them and check which nozzles came with your machine.

Fillers: carbon fibre, glass fibre

Short chopped fibre in the base polymer makes it stiffer, more dimensionally stable and less prone to creep, but more brittle, more anisotropic (fibres line up with the extrusion), and it abrades nozzles. Carbon-fibre parts are also electrically conductive, so keep chips and dust away from electronics.

3.4 Anisotropy: the most important printed-part fact

An FDM part is a stack of welded lines. Along a line the polymer chains are continuous and strong. Between layers the bond is a weld, which is weaker, and it is weakest at sharp corners and under tension. Rule of thumb: Z-direction tensile strength is roughly 30–70% of in-layer strength, depending on material, temperature (a hot chamber helps) and layer height. Good prints with a warm chamber and well-dried filament sit at the top of that range; poor ones fall below it.

This is a design decision before it is a material decision: orient every part so the dominant tension or bending stress runs along the layers. A tab printed standing up has the loads pulling layers apart. The same tab printed lying flat is several times stronger.

3.5 Heat: when does it stop being a mechanism?

Plastics soften gradually well below their melting point, losing stiffness fast near the glass transition (Tg). A part under load creeps and warps long before it melts. Check the worst temperature the part will see: sun on a window, a hot car, near a motor, a stepper driver or a heater, and the heat it makes itself (friction in a bearing or gear).

3.6 Moisture and drying

  • Many filaments absorb moisture from the air, notably nylon, PC, PVA and TPU, and to a lesser degree PETG and PLA. Wet filament pops and bubbles, prints rough and, importantly for mechanisms, prints weak, with poor layer bonding.
  • Typical drying (check your spool's label): PLA ≈ 45–55 °C, PETG ≈ 65 °C, ABS/ASA ≈ 80 °C, nylon ≈ 80+ °C for many hours. The built-in AMS 2 Pro dryer reportedly tops out near 65 °C, so nylon deserves a dedicated dryer, and printing it straight from the dry box.
  • Printed nylon parts absorb water after printing too: they swell slightly and get tougher but less stiff. Design clearances for the conditioned state, not the dry-off-the-printer state.

Do not forget the option of not printing a part: bearings, rods, springs, gears for high torque and fasteners are better bought. Chapter 15 is all about the print-plus-buy split.

3.8 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ What is the worst temperature, and is it above 80% of the material's practical limit?
  • ☐ Is the load sustained (creep) or cyclic (fatigue)? Pick PLA only if neither.
  • ☐ Does anything slide on printed plastic? Choose pairs: nylon on steel, or different polymers against each other.
  • ☐ Is the filament dry? Particularly nylon, PC, PVA, TPU.
  • ☐ Abrasive filament loaded: is the nozzle hardened?
  • Printing a functional hinge, clip or gear in PLA "because it printed so nicely", then watching it crack or creep.
  • Assuming datasheet strength applies to your part. Datasheets quote moulded samples or best-case in-layer prints.
  • Using wet nylon or PETG and blaming the design for weak layers.
  • Leaving PLA parts in a sunlit car.

3.9 Check yourself

A latch clip must hold a constant spring preload for years in a warm workshop. PLA or PETG?

PETG (or better, nylon or ASA). A constant load is the worst case for PLA's creep, and a warm workshop is closer to PLA's softening range than you'd think.

Why is a printed Z-direction tab at a corner such a bad idea?

The load pulls layers apart (weak direction) and the corner concentrates stress (chapter 2): two weaknesses stacked. Orient flat and add a fillet.

You switch a gear from PETG to PA-CF and it starts to cut the mating nylon gear's teeth. Why?

Carbon fibre makes the surface hard and abrasive. Pair a CF gear with a metal gear or pick unfilled nylon for both. Filler gives stiffness, not smooth sliding.
