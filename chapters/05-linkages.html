Linkages

Rigid bars joined by pins are the most forgiving mechanism for 3D printing: no teeth to mesh, no sliding surfaces except the pin, and they print flat. They turn rotation into rocking, rotation into straight-line pushing, short pulls into huge clamping forces, and they trace paths that no gear can. This chapter covers the main families, how to judge a linkage before you build it, and how to build one that doesn't wobble.

Chapter 1 introduced the four-bar and the Grashof condition. Here we use it. "Does it turn fully?" is the first question; the next is "does it push well?"

5.2 Transmission angle and dead centres

In a four-bar, the input link pushes the coupler, and the coupler pushes the output link. The angle between the coupler and the output link at their joint is the transmission angle μ. At μ = 90° the coupler pushes the output link squarely; its whole force goes into turning the output. As μ approaches 0° or 180°, the coupler pushes almost along the output link, so most of the force squeezes the pins instead of turning anything. At exactly 0° or 180° the mechanism is at a dead centre (a toggle position): it can jam, or the output can flip either way.

Practical rule: keep the transmission angle between about 40° and 140° over the working range (min(μ, 180°−μ) ≥ 40°). In 3D printing there's a second reason beyond efficiency: printed pins have friction and clearance, which at poor angles makes the mechanism stall or rattle at the dead-centre.

Typical fixes for a poor transmission angle: lengthen the output link or shorten the crank, move the ground pivots, or use a different linkage altogether. And if a dead centre is something you want (to lock a position), see the toggle in section 5.5.

5.3 The slider-crank

Replace the output rocker with a slider and you get the engine linkage: a crank of radius r, a connecting rod of length l, and a slider on a straight guide. The slider position is

x = r·cos θ + √( l² − (e − r·sin θ)² ) (e = offset of the guide from the crank axis)
Stroke ≈ 2r (exactly 2r when e = 0)

Notes for design:

  • The slider moves almost sinusoidally; it is faster at mid-stroke and slows to zero at each end, which is useful for pumps (smooth flow) and presses (slow near the bottom).
  • A longer rod (l/r large, 3.5–5 is common) is smoother and puts less sideways load on the guide. The sideways force on the slider is what wears printed guides.
  • Offsetting the guide from the crank axis makes the two strokes take unequal crank angles: a built-in quick-return, used in shapers and saws, where the working stroke is slow and the return is fast.

5.4 Straight lines and paths

A pin joint moves on a circle, so getting a straight line from pins alone is a classic problem. Linkages can solve it two ways:

  • Approximate straight line with a 4-bar: the path of a coupler point has a nearly straight section. Watt's linkage (two equal rockers) draws a figure-8 with a straight crossing. Hoeken's linkage (a crank-rocker with a coupler point extended to twice the coupler length) draws a D-shaped curve with a long straight edge. It is one of the best choices for a printable walking leg or a pick-and-place that needs a flat stroke.
  • Exact straight line with Peaucellier–Lipkin's seven-bar inversor: a clever rhombus of links that converts a circle into a perfect line. It needs 7 links and exact lengths; with printed slop the "perfect" line is lost, but it's a lovely illustration.

You can also design for a path other than a line. Coupler curves of a four-bar form a huge family (the Hrones–Nelson atlas catalogues them). In practice you don't synthesise by hand: you drag the joints in a linkage simulator or CAD motion study until the curve looks right, then check the transmission angle (5.2).

Walkers Walking toys use linkages that trace a "D" or a bean path with a flat bottom: Hoeken, Klann (a six-bar spider-leg linkage) and Jansen (an eleven-bar leg). They look complicated, but each is a four-bar plus a few extra links, and each leg runs off one crank. They print well because everything lies in parallel planes.

5.5 Toggles and over-centre linkages

When two links approach a straight line (a knee about to lock), a small force at the knee creates a very large force along the links. For a symmetric toggle with half-angle α measured from the line between the end pins:

F_out = F_in / (2·tan α)

As α → 0, the ideal force goes to infinity. In reality it is limited by elastic stretch of the links and play in the pins, so printed toggles clamp by flexing, and the click you feel as it passes over centre is the elastic energy it stores. That click is a feature: an over-centre linkage locks itself because pushing it back requires passing through the dead centre again.

Simulation 4 · Toggle force multiplier

Push the knee down with a fixed force and see how much the ends push. Compare α = 30° (about 0.9× the input force) with α = 5° (about 5.7×) and α = 1° (about 29×).

Printed toggles A toggle clamp with printed links will stretch, creep and crack if you load it near dead centre for long. Design the clamping to go a little past centre (a hard stop) so it holds against a stop, not on the unstable knife-edge, and put the highest-stress links in a stronger material, or use metal links with printed brackets.

5.6 Parallelograms, pantographs and scissors

  • Parallelogram linkage: two equal cranks and two equal links in a parallelogram keep the coupler parallel to the base as it moves along an arc. It's how a desk lamp keeps the head level, a drawer organiser tilts, and a robotic arm holds a platform flat. It is exact only if all four pin-to-pin lengths are exactly equal, and this is the mild over-constraint case we met in chapter 1. A double parallelogram (extra parallel link) adds stiffness against twisting.
  • Pantograph: a parallelogram with an extended arm, so a tracing point moves a similar shape scaled up or down by a fixed ratio. Used for scaling and for lifting: a scissor lift is a stack of crossed links, giving a large height range from a compact base.
  • Scissor and lazy-tongs mechanisms: crossed links pinned at their middles give a large extension. Loads multiply: stress in the pins gets very high near the retracted position, and each stage amplifies slop, so printed scissors get wobbly if tall.

5.7 Designing a linkage: a practical workflow

  1. State the motion: input type (crank, lever), output (angle, stroke, path), and the loads.
  2. Pick a family from the table at the top. A four-bar covers most rocking jobs; a slider-crank covers straight push/pull.
  3. Sketch the layout in 2D with a drag-and-simulate tool: a free web linkage simulator, your CAD's motion-study feature, or a quick script. Change one length at a time and watch the output.
  4. Check the quality numbers: transmission angle (> 40°), dead centres (avoid, or put a stop), range of motion, and Grashof type.
  5. Check collisions: links in the same plane cross each other. Put links on different layers (offset planes with washers/spacers) so they pass by one another.
  6. Add stops at the ends of the motion so the mechanism can't be forced past a dead centre or locked.
  7. Print a cardboard or thin-PLA mock-up before the final: it takes ten minutes and reveals the interferences.

5.8 Building linkages from printed parts

The pivot

The pivot is the part that matters. Options from quick to proper (detailed in chapters 10 and 13):

  • Screw and nut (M3/M4) through the links with a washer or a printed spacer, tightened until the links just spin freely. Quick, but the thread can chew a soft hole: add a sleeve (a short length of brass tube, a printed bushing or a shoulder screw).
  • Shoulder screw (a bolt with a smooth unthreaded shank): the smooth shoulder is a real bearing surface. The cleanest cheap pivot.
  • Printed pin or print-in-place pin: works for light duty. Print the pin lying down for strength.
  • Ball bearings (e.g. 623 or 608 sizes) pressed into the links for low friction and low play; best for long linkages and high loads.

Making links that don't flop

  • A link in the plane of motion is a beam in bending or a strut in compression. Make links deeper than they are wide (in the plane): remember h³ stiffness (chapter 2). Add ribs, not thickness.
  • Long links in compression buckle. Where possible arrange the long links so they are in tension, or shorten them, or use a tubular/rod link.
  • Side loads twist a link about its length, which bends its pins out of plane. A clevis (a fork around a tongue, so the pin is loaded in double shear and the load is centred) is much stiffer than a single-lap joint that loads the pin at an offset.
  • Print links flat on the bed, with pin holes vertical: the layers run along the link (strong in bending, as chapter 4 says), and the holes come out round.

Tolerances and play

  • Pin-hole centre-to-centre distance is the critical dimension, not the hole size. A 0.2 mm error in a 30 mm crank changes the stroke by about 1%; the same error in a short coupler near a dead centre can stop the mechanism.
  • Play at each pivot adds up; at the output it is multiplied by the lever ratio. Keep pin clearance small (sliding fit from your coupon, chapter 4) and put a preload (a spring or elastic band) on the output if you need a precise position.
  • Use slotted holes or an adjustable link (a turnbuckle or two-part link with a clamp) at one pivot so you can tune the geometry after printing.
  • Use washers or spacers between rubbing links to avoid friction on the flat faces, and a dot of grease or silicone oil.

5.9 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Grashof checked: does the input link turn fully?
  • ☐ Minimum transmission angle ≥ 40° over the working range.
  • ☐ Dead centres either avoided or deliberately used with a stop.
  • ☐ Links in different planes where they cross; spacers fitted.
  • ☐ Long compression links checked for buckling.
  • ☐ Pivots chosen on purpose (shoulder screw, bearing); clevis where the load is high.
  • ☐ An end stop at each limit; links printed flat.
  • Placing a pivot just a few mm from the dead centre, then wondering why it jams.
  • Using a thin printed link as a long strut under compression.
  • Forgetting that links in the same plane collide.
  • Expecting a parallelogram or pantograph to hold geometry when every pin has 0.3 mm of play.

5.10 Check yourself

A four-bar has a minimum transmission angle of 12° when run full circle. What are two ways to improve it?

Shorten the crank or lengthen the output link, so the coupler meets the output link nearer to 90°; or shift the ground pivots. Then check Grashof is still satisfied. Simulation 1's "Poor" preset is a good place to practise.

You need a clamp that holds its position without power. Which linkage idea gives that?

An over-centre toggle: go slightly past the dead centre so the load tries to push it into a stop, locking it. Release means passing back through dead centre.

A slider-crank has r = 20 mm and a 4 mm guide offset. What is the approximate stroke, and what changes compared to the zero-offset version?

The stroke stays almost exactly 2r = 40 mm (a 4 mm offset changes it by well under 1 mm), but the forward and return strokes take unequal crank angles: a quick-return effect.
