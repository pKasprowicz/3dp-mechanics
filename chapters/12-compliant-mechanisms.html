Compliant mechanisms and living hinges

Every mechanism so far moved because separate parts slid or rolled against each other. A compliant mechanism moves because a single part bends. There is no pin, no bore, no backlash, no wear, and no assembly: print it in one go, and it works. This is where FDM printing shines, because making a thin flexible web next to a thick rigid block is free. It also comes with one hard constraint, strain, that decides whether the part lasts one flex or a million.

12.1 Why compliant

AdvantageCost
One part: no assembly, no fasteners, no clearance to tuneLimited travel: you can only bend so far
No friction, no backlash, no lubricant, no wear in the jointMotion needs force, because bending stores energy in the part (it acts like a spring)
Repeatable, smooth motion; no stick-slipFatigue and creep: plastics tire and relax
Easy to miniaturiseStiffness depends on material, temperature and layer quality
Built-in spring return and detentOff-axis stiffness is limited: flexures twist and wobble

Use compliant elements for: hinges on lids and clips, snap-fit latches, spring returns, precision micro-adjusters, grippers, buttons and bistable switches, and anywhere a printed pin hinge would wear out.

12.2 The beam rules that matter

All the flexures here are variations of the beam of chapter 2. With width b and thickness t (the dimension in the bending direction), the second moment of area is I = b·t³/12. The three results you need:

Strain in a bent strip ε = t · κ / 2 (κ = curvature, 1/radius)
Uniform bend through angle θ over length L: ε = t · θ / (2L)
End-loaded cantilever, deflection δ: F = 3EI·δ / L³, ε_max = 3·t·δ / (2L²)
Bending stiffness of a hinge strip: M = E·I·θ / L

The point: strain depends on thickness over radius. A thinner strip, or a longer strip bent through the same angle, gives a lower strain. Doubling t doubles the strain but multiplies stiffness by 8 (t³). To make a flexible hinge, make it thin and long, not short and chunky.

Strain limits of printed plastics

Every flexure design is a strain budget. Rough guide values for printed parts (single bend to failure, and repeated cycling for thousands of flexes); the cyclic allowance is much lower because of fatigue:

MaterialSingle flex, max strainCyclic, max strainComment
PLA~1.5%~0.5%Brittle: avoid for any repeated flexing
PETG~4%~1.5%Decent hinges, snap-fits
ABS / ASA~3%~1%OK
Nylon (PA)~8%~3%The best rigid printed flexure material
PA-CF~2%~0.7%Stiff: good for small-travel flexures, not hinges
TPU~40%~15%For large motion, low force, rubbery feel

These are indicative guide values: the real limit depends on print quality, layer orientation, temperature and the thickness of the hinge. Always print a test strip and flex it until it dies. And note the consequence for living hinges: bending a 0.5 mm strip through 90° over 3 mm gives ε = 0.5 × 1.57 / (2 × 3) = 13%: far beyond any rigid printed plastic's limit. Short, thick printed "living hinges" break; long thin ones survive. (Injection-moulded polypropylene hinges tolerate ~30% because the polymer chains align on the first bend: a trick you can't copy in FDM.)

Spread the bend

If the angle needed is large and the strip can't be long, split the bend across several hinges in a row (a series of thin grooves). Each takes θ/n, and the strain per groove falls by n. A segmented "box lid" strip, a multi-fold hinge, and a flexible spine all rely on this.

Printing a living hinge

  • Print the strip flat on the bed with its length in the XY plane: the strain runs along the extruded lines, the strong direction (chapter 4). Never print a strip standing so that bending strains the layer bonds.
  • Thickness 0.4–0.8 mm (two to four extrusion lines, or two to four layers if flat); hinge length ≥ 8–10 mm for 90° in PETG, shorter for small angles; no infill in the strip, only perimeters.
  • Place the seam away from the hinge; a seam is a weak spot at the exact location of highest strain.
  • Taper the hinge into the rigid blocks with generous fillets so the strain doesn't concentrate at the edge (chapter 2).
  • Rest angle: a printed bent strip has zero strain where it was printed. Print the hinge in the position it is held in most often (for a box, closed or half-open) so it sits relaxed, rather than storing a permanent strain that makes it creep.
  • Use PETG or nylon, not PLA. Print slower and a bit hotter for the best layer bonding.

Pseudo-rigid-body model: use your linkage knowledge

A flexible cantilever bent by an end force behaves approximately like a rigid link of length γL (γ ≈ 0.85) connected to the base by a pin with a torsional spring, K ≈ 2.25·EI/L. This lets you treat compliant mechanisms like the linkages of chapter 5: draw the pivots, add springs, and check the geometry. It is the quickest path from an idea ("a gripper that closes by squeezing a flexible frame") to dimensions.

The parallel flexure

Two leaf springs of length L, thickness t, width b, joined between a base and a shuttle, give a nearly straight linear motion with a spring-like stiffness:

k = 24·E·I / L³ = 2·E·b·t³ / L³ (both leaves)
Strain at the leaf ends ε = 3·t·δ / L²
Parasitic shortening ≈ 3·δ² / (5·L) (the shuttle also moves a little toward the base)

12.4 Snap-fits

A snap-fit is a cantilever with a hook: it deflects as the mating part pushes past, then springs back and holds. The same strain rule decides the design. For a straight cantilever of constant thickness, the strain at the root for a hook that must deflect by y is

ε = 1.5 · t · y / L² (tapering the thickness to half at the tip: ε ≈ 0.92 · t · y / L²)
Deflection force F = 3·E·I·y / L³
Mating (push-on) force W = F · (μ + tan α) / (1 − μ · tan α) (α = lead-in angle of the hook)
  • Make it longer, or taper it. Length enters squared in the strain: doubling L cuts ε by 4. A tapered arm spreads the strain evenly along the beam instead of concentrating it at the root.
  • Fillet the root (radius ≥ 0.5·t). A sharp root is where printed snap-fits break.
  • Print flat: the arm's length lies in the XY plane so layers run along it.
  • Lead-in angle controls the force to snap on (30°: gentle; 60°: hard). The return angle controls whether it releases: a 90° return face locks permanently, 45° lets it be pulled off.
  • Permanent vs. repeated use: a snap-fit used once can use the single-flex limit; one used often must obey the cyclic limit.
  • Test the fit after a few dozen cycles: printed hooks creep and loosen.

12.5 Springs from plastic

  • Printed springs are good for light, short-travel, occasional use and bad for permanent preload. Plastic creeps: a PLA spring compressed for a few weeks has lost much of its force. Nylon and PETG do better, and TPU springs are soft but long-lived.
  • The best printed springs are bending springs printed flat: leaf springs, zigzag/serpentine strips, spiral springs and flexure beams. A printed coil spring loads the wire in torsion across layer boundaries, a weak mode with short life.
  • For constant forces, buy a spring. Steel springs cost cents, do not creep, and have well-known rates (chapter 13).
  • Use printed springs where the spring is part of the mechanism (a returning button, a latch) and the load is modest; in that case design for the cyclic strain limit, as above.

12.6 Design workflow

  1. Sketch the motion as a rigid-body linkage (chapter 5), then replace pivots by thin sections or flexible beams.
  2. Pick the material from the strain limit and the force you need.
  3. Size the flexing member with the beam formulas: choose t for a strain well below the limit at the largest deflection; choose L from the force and the stiffness.
  4. Check the stiffness: the actuation force is the spring force of the flexure; make sure your input (a finger, a motor, a spring) can deliver it.
  5. Check the unwanted motions: out-of-plane stiffness (increase b, add a second flexure), buckling in compression (shorten, thicken), parasitic shortening.
  6. Print a test coupon and flex it to failure; adjust t and L. Print the final part in the same orientation and settings.

12.8 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Strain checked: ε = tθ/2L (hinge) or 1.5ty/L² (snap-fit) vs the cyclic limit.
  • ☐ Material: PETG or nylon for flexures. PLA only for single-use.
  • ☐ Printed flat, strain along the layers; seam away from the flexing area.
  • ☐ Fillets at every flexure root.
  • ☐ Printed at the rest position; no permanent strain.
  • ☐ Stiffness (force needed) and off-axis stiffness checked.
  • ☐ A test coupon flexed to failure.
  • Copying a polypropylene living hinge from a food container into FDM PLA.
  • Making the hinge short "for neatness".
  • Printing a snap-fit hook standing up.
  • Holding a PLA spring under constant load.

12.9 Check yourself

A 0.5 mm PETG strip must bend 45° repeatedly. With a cyclic limit of 1.5%, how long must the hinge be?

θ = 0.785 rad. L = t·θ / (2ε) = 0.5 × 0.785 / (2 × 0.015) = 13 mm. A 3 mm hinge would reach 6.5% strain: it would last a handful of flexes at best.

A snap-fit arm 15 mm long and 1.5 mm thick must deflect 1 mm. Strain? Is PLA acceptable for a part clipped on only once?

ε = 1.5·1.5·1 / 15² = 2.25/225 = 1.0%. PLA's single-flex limit is about 1.5%, so it would survive one-time assembly but not repeated use (cyclic limit 0.5%). PETG (cyclic limit 1.5%) is comfortable.

You double the thickness of a flexure leaf to stop it twisting. What happens to its stiffness and strain?

Stiffness ∝ t³: eight times stiffer. Strain at a given deflection ∝ t: twice as high. So you need eight times the force and you are closer to the strain limit. Widening the leaf (b) raises the out-of-plane stiffness (∝ b³) with only a linear increase in the working stiffness, and is usually the better fix.
