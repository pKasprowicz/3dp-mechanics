Actuation basics

A mechanism is only a way of shaping force and motion. Something still has to supply it: a hand, a motor, a spring, a solenoid. Choosing the actuator and matching it to the load is where most beginners either overbuild (a huge motor for a tiny lid) or under-build (a servo that stalls and cooks). This chapter gives you the types, the numbers that matter, and three calculators for sizing.

16.2 Torque, speed and power

Every rotating actuator trades speed for torque. Power is the product (P = T·ω, with ω in rad/s = rpm × 2π/60), and a motor has a limited amount of it. A gearbox changes the balance but not the power (minus losses). To pick an actuator you need the torque at the output and the speed at the output for the real motion.

  • Holding torque: the torque needed to keep the load still (gravity on an arm, a spring preload).
  • Running torque: friction, cutting, pushing a load at constant speed.
  • Acceleration torque: T = J·α (inertia × angular acceleration). Often the largest term for fast moves, and the one beginners forget.
  • Add them, then add a safety margin of 1.5–2× (motors rarely deliver the datasheet maximum, and friction in printed mechanisms is higher than you think).

16.3 DC motors and gearmotors

A brushed DC motor has a nearly straight torque–speed line: maximum speed at zero torque (no-load speed) and maximum torque at zero speed (stall). Current is proportional to torque, so stall draws a large current and heats the windings. The motor is happy at about 20–30% of stall torque continuously; above that it runs hot, and at stall for more than a few seconds it can burn out.

A gearmotor puts a gearbox in front: output torque ≈ motor torque × ratio × efficiency, output speed ≈ motor speed ÷ ratio. Small gearmotors (N20, TT, 37D) have plastic or metal gears and a ratio from 10:1 to 1000:1; pick the ratio from the speed you need, then check the torque.

16.4 Stepper motors

A stepper moves in fixed increments (typically 1.8° = 200 steps per revolution); a driver sequences the coils. Microstepping (÷ 16, ÷ 32...) smooths the motion and gives finer commanded steps. Features and traps:

  • Open loop: no feedback. If the load torque exceeds the motor torque, it silently loses steps and your position is wrong.
  • Torque falls with speed. At low speed the motor delivers its holding torque, but the coils' inductance stops the current from rising fast enough at high step rates, so the pull-out torque drops. Higher supply voltage and lower inductance raise the usable speed. The curve is on the motor datasheet.
  • Always powered: at standstill a stepper draws its set current and gets warm. Reduce current at rest; a stepper at 60–80 °C is normal.
  • Resonance at certain speeds (a rumble or a skip). Fixes: microstepping, a damper, a different speed, an interesting mass on the shaft, stealth modes on modern drivers.
  • Drivers: A4988/DRV8825 (basic), TMC2209-class (quiet, current-sensing, stall detection). Set the current to the motor's rated value or lower, and cool the driver.

16.5 Servos

A hobby servo contains a DC motor, a gearbox, a potentiometer and a control board. You send a pulse (1–2 ms every 20 ms) and it goes to the corresponding angle (usually 0–180°) and holds. Cheap, compact and perfect for small arms, grippers and flaps, with traps:

  • The stall torque in the specification is not usable torque. Derate to 50% for continuous holding; the gears are small and the motor heats.
  • Torque is quoted in kg·cm: the weight in kilograms that the servo can hold on a horn of 1 cm (convert: 1 kg·cm = 0.098 N·m).
  • Plastic gear trains strip under shock. Don't hit the servo's end of travel against a stop; use metal gears or a slip clutch if the load can shock it.
  • A servo holding against a load draws power continuously and can buzz; add mechanical advantage or a counterbalance so it isn't holding at its limit.
  • Power the servo from a separate supply capable of the stall current (0.5–2 A for a standard servo), with shared ground.

16.6 Solenoids, linear actuators and others

  • Solenoid: force falls rapidly with stroke (inversely with the square of the gap). Use it at its short end, with a return spring. Duty cycle matters: a solenoid rated for 25% duty burns out if held on. A "hold" current lower than the pull-in current (via PWM) reduces heat.
  • Linear actuator: motor + leadscrew (chapter 9): force = 2πηT/lead; self-locking at low lead. Check the speed.
  • Pneumatics: force = pressure × area (1 bar on a 10 mm piston = 8 N). Gentle, simple and light; a small compressor or a syringe pushes a soft gripper.
  • Muscle by hand: for hand-operated parts design for a comfortable force: a finger push of 5–15 N, a hand crank of 10–20 N at ~150 mm radius (1.5–3 N·m).

16.7 Controls and electronics in one page

  • Driver / H-bridge between the microcontroller and any motor; never power a motor directly from a pin. Add a flyback diode on solenoids and relays.
  • Limit switches or hall sensors at the ends of travel, for homing and for safety; encoders if you need position feedback from a DC motor.
  • Supply: size it for the peak current, with a bulk capacitor near the driver; keep motor wiring away from sensor wiring.
  • Soft limits and slip clutches in the mechanism protect the printed parts from a controller mistake (chapter 14).
  • Thermal check: after a long run, touch the motor and driver. If they are too hot to hold, reduce current or duty cycle.

16.9 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Load torque estimated: holding + running + acceleration, with a 1.5–2× margin.
  • ☐ Speed at the output specified; gear ratio chosen from it.
  • ☐ Motor within its continuous range (≤ 25% stall for brushed DC, ≤ 50% rated for servos).
  • ☐ Supply sized for peak current; heat checked.
  • ☐ Stops and a torque limiter protect the printed mechanism.
  • ☐ Reaction torque path (mounting) is stiff.
  • Sizing a motor from its stall torque instead of its continuous torque.
  • Forgetting that stepper torque drops with speed.
  • Holding an arm with a servo at full extension all day.
  • Driving a motor straight from a microcontroller pin.

16.10 Check yourself

A servo is rated 5 kg·cm. What mass can it hold continuously at the end of a 10 cm arm held horizontal?

Derate to 50%: 2.5 kg·cm. Mass = 2.5 kg·cm / 10 cm = 0.25 kg at the tip, less the weight of the arm itself.

A brushed motor has stall torque 20 mN·m and no-load speed 10 000 rpm. You gear it 50:1 at 60% efficiency. What are the output stall torque and the no-load output speed?

Output stall = 0.02 × 50 × 0.6 = 0.6 N·m. No-load output speed = 10 000 / 50 = 200 rpm. Continuous use at ~25% of stall means about 0.15 N·m at ~150 rpm.

Why does a stepper that works fine at 100 rpm skip steps at 600 rpm with the same load?

Its pull-out torque falls with speed (coil inductance limits the current rise per step), so at 600 rpm it may deliver less torque than the load needs. Fix: higher supply voltage, a motor with lower inductance, lighter load, or gentler acceleration.
