Actuation basics

A mechanism is only a way of shaping force and motion. Something still has to supply it: a hand, a motor, a spring, a solenoid. Choosing the actuator and matching it to the load is where most beginners either overbuild (a huge motor for a tiny lid) or under-build (a servo that stalls and cooks). This chapter gives you the types, the numbers that matter, and three calculators for sizing.

Rules of thumb: steppers for precise positioning without feedback, servos for angles, geared DC motors for continuous rotation, solenoids for brief taps, hands for everything that doesn't have to be automatic.

16.2 Torque, speed and power

Every rotating actuator trades speed for torque. Power is the product (P = T·ω, with ω in rad/s = rpm × 2π/60), and a motor has a limited amount of it. A gearbox changes the balance but not the power (minus losses). To pick an actuator you need the torque at the output and the speed at the output for the real motion.

  • Holding torque: the torque needed to keep the load still (gravity on an arm, a spring preload).
  • Running torque: friction, cutting, pushing a load at constant speed.
  • Acceleration torque: T = J·α (inertia × angular acceleration). Often the largest term for fast moves, and the one beginners forget.
  • Add them, then add a safety margin of 1.5–2× (motors rarely deliver the datasheet maximum, and friction in printed mechanisms is higher than you think).

16.3 DC motors and gearmotors

A brushed DC motor has a nearly straight torque–speed line: maximum speed at zero torque (no-load speed) and maximum torque at zero speed (stall). Current is proportional to torque, so stall draws a large current and heats the windings. The motor is happy at about 20–30% of stall torque continuously; above that it runs hot, and at stall for more than a few seconds it can burn out.

Why the line is straight. Two effects, both from the same magnet and coil. Current in the coil produces torque in proportion, T = k·I. And a coil spinning in the magnet's field acts as a generator, producing a back-EMF that opposes the supply in proportion to speed, E = k·ω (the same k). The supply voltage is shared between that back-EMF and the coil's resistance: V = I·R + k·ω. Standing still, there is no back-EMF, so the full voltage drives current through the small resistance: maximum current, maximum torque (stall). Spinning freely, the back-EMF almost equals the supply, so very little current flows: maximum speed, almost no torque. In between, speed falls in a straight line as the load torque rises.

  • Voltage moves the line: double the voltage and both the no-load speed and the stall torque double. PWM speed control works by lowering the average voltage.
  • The load sets the speed: the motor settles where the load torque meets the line, which is what Simulation 1 shows.
  • Peak mechanical power is at half the stall torque and half the no-load speed, but the losses there are high; run lighter for efficiency and life.

A gearmotor puts a gearbox in front: output torque ≈ motor torque × ratio × efficiency, output speed ≈ motor speed ÷ ratio. Small gearmotors (N20, TT, 37D) have plastic or metal gears and a ratio from 10:1 to 1000:1; pick the ratio from the speed you need, then check the torque.

16.4 Stepper motors

A stepper moves in fixed increments (typically 1.8° = 200 steps per revolution); a driver sequences the coils. Microstepping (÷ 16, ÷ 32...) smooths the motion and gives finer commanded steps.

How it steps. A hybrid stepper has two coils (phases A and B) wound on the stator poles, and a permanent-magnet rotor. Whatever currents flow in A and B add up to a magnetic field pointing in one direction, and the rotor turns until it lines up with that field, like a compass needle. The driver moves the field by changing the two currents: full steps switch them between +I and −I in a fixed sequence, and microstepping sets them to sine and cosine values (I·cos φ in A, I·sin φ in B), which points the field at any angle φ in between. A real 1.8° motor has 50 small teeth on its rotor, so the rotor turns only 1/50 of the field's rotation: one full electrical turn of the field moves the shaft 7.2°, four full steps.

A load on the shaft pulls the rotor slightly behind the field, like stretching a spring. The restoring torque grows as the rotor lags, up to the holding torque at a lag of one full step. Beyond that, the rotor cannot hold and falls back to the next stable position, a whole rotor tooth (four full steps) behind: a lost step, which an open-loop driver never notices. Microstepping divides the commanded position finely, but under load the rotor still lags by the same angle, so the real accuracy of a microstep is much coarser than its size.

Simulation 2 · Inside a stepper motor

A simplified two-phase stepper with a two-pole rotor (a real 1.8° motor repeats this pattern 50 times around its rotor, so its shaft moves 1/50 as far). The coil currents (bars and lower plot) set the field direction (dashed arrow); the rotor (orange) follows. Choose the stepping mode and step it, then add load torque and see the rotor lag behind the field, and slip once the load exceeds the holding torque.

Features and traps:

  • Open loop: no feedback. If the load torque exceeds the motor torque, it silently loses steps and your position is wrong.
  • Torque falls with speed. At low speed the motor delivers its holding torque, but the coils' inductance stops the current from rising fast enough at high step rates, so the pull-out torque drops. Higher supply voltage and lower inductance raise the usable speed. The curve is on the motor datasheet.
  • Always powered: at standstill a stepper draws its set current and gets warm. Reduce current at rest; a stepper at 60–80 °C is normal.
  • Resonance at certain speeds (a rumble or a skip). Fixes: microstepping, a damper, a different speed, an interesting mass on the shaft, stealth modes on modern drivers.
  • Drivers: A4988/DRV8825 (basic), TMC2209-class (quiet, current-sensing, stall detection). Set the current to the motor's rated value or lower, and cool the driver.

16.5 Servos

A hobby servo contains a DC motor, a gearbox, a potentiometer and a control board. You send a pulse (1–2 ms every 20 ms) and it goes to the corresponding angle (usually 0–180°) and holds.

How it holds an angle: a feedback loop. The potentiometer is geared to the output shaft and measures where it actually is. The control board compares that with the angle the pulse asks for, and drives the motor in the direction that reduces the difference, harder the bigger the difference (proportional control). As the output approaches the target, the error and the drive fall to zero and it stops. Push on the arm and it is pushed off target; the error grows and the motor pushes back. Three consequences: a servo holding a load is always slightly off target (it needs some error to produce torque); it draws current the whole time it resists a load; and if the loop is tuned too aggressively, or the load is springy or has backlash, it overshoots back and forth around the target, which you hear as buzzing or see as jitter. A small dead band around the target stops it chasing tiny errors.

Cheap, compact and perfect for small arms, grippers and flaps, with traps:

  • The stall torque in the specification is not usable torque. Derate to 50% for continuous holding; the gears are small and the motor heats.
  • Torque is quoted in kg·cm: the weight in kilograms that the servo can hold on a horn of 1 cm (convert: 1 kg·cm = 0.098 N·m).
  • Plastic gear trains strip under shock. Don't hit the servo's end of travel against a stop; use metal gears or a slip clutch if the load can shock it.
  • A servo holding against a load draws power continuously and can buzz; add mechanical advantage or a counterbalance so it isn't holding at its limit.
  • Power the servo from a separate supply capable of the stall current (0.5–2 A for a standard servo), with shared ground.

16.6 Solenoids, linear actuators and others

Solenoids

A solenoid pulls an iron plunger into a coil. It is fast, cheap and simple, but its force falls steeply with the gap (roughly with the inverse square), so the force at full stroke can be a tenth of the force when closed. Use it at the short end of its stroke, with a return spring sized below the solenoid's force at the open position. A small 12 V push-pull solenoid gives a few newtons at 5 mm and tens of newtons near closed.

  • Duty cycle matters: a solenoid rated for 25% duty burns out if held on. To hold it closed, drop to a lower hold current with PWM after pull-in (the closed magnetic circuit needs much less), or use a latching solenoid (a permanent magnet holds it; a reverse pulse releases it).
  • Add a flyback diode across the coil, and expect a sharp impact at the end of the stroke: give the plunger a TPU or rubber bumper and keep printed parts out of the hammer path.
  • To get more stroke, use a lever (chapter 2): the solenoid works near its strong closed end, and the lever multiplies the motion.

Linear actuators

A bought linear actuator is a gearmotor and a leadscrew in a tube: force = 2π·η·T / lead, self-locking at low lead (chapter 9). They give tens to thousands of newtons at slow speeds (5–50 mm/s), usually with built-in end switches. Mount them on clevis pins at both ends so they can pivot: an actuator clamped rigidly is side-loaded and wrecks its seals and nut. Micro linear actuators (with a small stepper or a geared motor and an M3-size screw) suit printed mechanisms well.

Pneumatics

Force is pressure times area: F = p · A, and 1 bar is 0.1 N/mm², so 1 bar on a 10 mm piston gives 8 N and 6 bar on a 20 mm bore cylinder gives about 190 N. Pneumatics are fast, gentle (air is springy), light at the moving end and safe around fingers at low pressure. A small aquarium pump or a syringe drives soft pneumatic grippers printed in TPU. Printed cylinders leak through layer lines unless sealed (an O-ring piston in a reamed or smoothed bore, or a coated print); bought mini cylinders are cheap and reliable.

Others worth knowing

  • Shape-memory alloy wire (nitinol): contracts about 3–5% when heated by a current, silently, with useful force for its size. Tiny strokes (lever them up), slow to cool, low efficiency: good for small silent latches and toys.
  • Voice-coil and magnet actuators: a coil in a magnet's field gives a force proportional to current, with no friction; for fast, short, proportional motion.
  • Permanent magnets as springs, latches and detents: the force rises sharply as the gap closes, which suits a "snap shut" latch (chapter 14).
  • Muscle: for hand-operated parts, design for a comfortable force: a finger push of 5–15 N, a thumb on a button of 2–5 N, a hand crank of 10–20 N at ~150 mm radius (1.5–3 N·m), a firm hand squeeze of 50–100 N.

16.7 Controls and electronics in one page

A driver for every motor

Never power a motor directly from a microcontroller pin: put a driver between them, sized for the motor's stall current.

Knowing where it is

  • Limit switches or hall sensors at the ends of travel, for homing and for safety; encoders if you need position feedback from a DC motor.
  • A homing routine for any stepper axis: move toward the switch at moderate speed until it triggers, back off a few millimetres, approach again slowly, and set that position as zero. The second, slow approach makes the zero repeatable to a fraction of a millimetre.
  • Mount switches so the mechanism hits a hard stop shortly after the switch, never the switch body itself, and so the switch lever is pressed, not crushed.

Power and wiring

  • Supply: size it for the peak current of everything running at once, with a bulk capacitor (100–470 µF) near each driver.
  • Keep motor wiring away from sensor wiring, twist motor pairs together, and use a common ground.
  • Strain relief: every cable that moves must be clamped on both sides of the moving joint, so the solder joints and connectors never take the bending.
  • Thermal check: after a long run, touch the motor and driver. If they are too hot to hold, reduce current or duty cycle.
  • Soft limits and slip clutches in the mechanism protect the printed parts from a controller mistake (chapter 14).

Cables that move: cable chains

Wires to a moving carriage or arm break after a few thousand bends if they flex at a sharp point. Spread the bend over a defined radius:

  • Cable chain (drag chain, energy chain): a chain of hinged links that carries the cables and bends only one way, at a fixed minimum radius. Printable as snap-together links (print several in place, joined by pin hinges with 0.3–0.4 mm clearance) or bought cheaply. The bend radius should be at least 7–10× the thickest cable's diameter. Fix both ends; the moving end travels on the axis at half the chain's length, so plan the space for the loop.
  • Flat flexible cable or ribbon cable in a long, gentle loop: good for short strokes in one plane.
  • Spiral wrap or a sprung loop: cheap, for slow, short motion.
  • Use stranded, high-flex wire (silicone-insulated, fine strands), never solid core. Rotating joints that must turn continuously need a slip ring.

16.9 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Load torque estimated: holding + running + acceleration, with a 1.5–2× margin.
  • ☐ Speed at the output specified; gear ratio chosen from it.
  • ☐ Motor within its continuous range (≤ 25% stall for brushed DC, ≤ 50% rated for servos).
  • ☐ Supply sized for peak current; heat checked.
  • ☐ Stops and a torque limiter protect the printed mechanism.
  • ☐ Reaction torque path (mounting) is stiff.
  • Sizing a motor from its stall torque instead of its continuous torque.
  • Forgetting that stepper torque drops with speed.
  • Holding an arm with a servo at full extension all day.
  • Driving a motor straight from a microcontroller pin.

16.10 Check yourself

A servo is rated 5 kg·cm. What mass can it hold continuously at the end of a 10 cm arm held horizontal?

Derate to 50%: 2.5 kg·cm. Mass = 2.5 kg·cm / 10 cm = 0.25 kg at the tip, less the weight of the arm itself.

A brushed motor has stall torque 20 mN·m and no-load speed 10 000 rpm. You gear it 50:1 at 60% efficiency. What are the output stall torque and the no-load output speed?

Output stall = 0.02 × 50 × 0.6 = 0.6 N·m. No-load output speed = 10 000 / 50 = 200 rpm. Continuous use at ~25% of stall means about 0.15 N·m at ~150 rpm.

Why does a stepper that works fine at 100 rpm skip steps at 600 rpm with the same load?

Its pull-out torque falls with speed (coil inductance limits the current rise per step), so at 600 rpm it may deliver less torque than the load needs. Fix: higher supply voltage, a motor with lower inductance, lighter load, or gentler acceleration.
