Belts, chains and flexible drives

When two shafts are far apart, gears become a pile of large wheels. A loop of belt, chain or cable does the same job with a fraction of the material, forgives misalignment, and is quiet. Flexible drives are also the easiest way to turn a motor's rotation into a long, fast linear motion, which is why your printer's own axes run on belts.

8.2 Timing belts

A timing belt is a reinforced rubber belt with teeth that engage grooves in a pulley. It cannot slip, so the speed ratio is the tooth ratio. The belt's strength is in its embedded fibre cords (glass, aramid or steel), which is why a thin belt can pull so hard without stretching.

ProfilePitchWhere you meet it
GT22 mm3D printers, small CNC, camera sliders; the hobby standard (belts 6 mm and 9 mm wide)
GT3 / HTD 3M3 mmSmall robots, more torque than GT2
HTD 5M / GT55 mmHeavier machines; large enough that printed pulleys are practical
T2.5, T5, XL, MXL2.5, 5, 5.08, 2.032 mmOlder profiles, inch series (XL, MXL)

Always match belt and pulley profile (a GT2 belt on an MXL pulley fits sloppily and wears out). Belt length is quoted as a tooth count (GT2-300 = 150 teeth × 2 mm = 300 mm). Pulleys have a bore (5 mm or 8 mm), a tooth count (16–20 is common), and often flanges to keep the belt on.

The geometry you need

Pitch diameter d = z · p / π (p = tooth pitch, z = teeth)
Ratio i = z₂ / z₁
Belt length L ≈ 2C + π(D + d)/2 + (D − d)² / (4C)
Wrap angle θ = π − 2·asin((D − d)/(2C)) (small pulley)
Teeth in mesh z₁ · θ / 2π (want ≥ 6 for a loaded small pulley)

A small pulley means the belt bends tightly (shorter belt life) and has fewer teeth engaged (more risk of skipping). For GT2, use 16–20 teeth or more for the driving pulley.

Tension: tight enough, not tighter

  • A timing belt needs pre-tension so the slack side doesn't go slack under load and the teeth stay seated. Too loose: tooth skipping, backlash, flapping. Too tight: belt wear, bearing wear, noise, and printed parts bending.
  • Always provide a way to tension: a slotted motor mount, an eccentric or a spring-loaded idler, a tension screw, a tensioner plate. Never design a fixed centre distance and hope the belt length matches.
  • Measuring: pluck the free span like a guitar string. Natural frequency f = (1/2L)·√(T/m′) (L = span length, T = tension, m′ = belt mass per unit length from the datasheet), so you can set tension by ear or with a phone frequency app.
  • Idlers on the toothed side should be toothed (or at least as big as the minimum pulley); a smooth idler on the back of the belt can be small but should still have diameter > ~20 mm for GT2.

8.3 Making belts work with printed parts

  • Buy the pulleys for GT2: metal 16/20T pulleys with grub screws are cheap and precise. Printed GT2 teeth are 0.75 mm features: unreliable. For HTD 5M and larger, printed pulleys work, and so do V-grooves and flat crowned pulleys.
  • Belt clamps: to attach a belt to a carriage, print a clamp with a toothed bed matching the belt (a GT2 clamp is printable at 0.1 mm layers with a fine nozzle setting), clamp the two ends overlapping teeth-to-teeth, or loop both ends with a printed block and screw.
  • Alignment: pulleys must be in the same plane (to within about 1 mm) and shafts parallel. Misalignment makes the belt climb flanges and walk off. Print the shaft supports in a single part when you can (chapter 4).
  • Route belt runs parallel to the motion axis on linear drives, and keep both runs of the belt at the same height, or the carriage will twist.
  • Flat belts (and round belts or rubber bands): crown the pulley, i.e. make its face slightly convex (a 1–2° taper from the centre), and the belt self-centres. Flanges on both sides fight the belt instead.
  • Stiff frame: belt tension is a continuous force on your printed parts. A 6 mm GT2 belt at printer tension pulls with tens of newtons on each pulley; a thin PLA bracket will bow over a few weeks (creep, chapter 2). Use thick walls, ribs and PETG or nylon.

8.4 Linear belt drives

A belt over a pair of pulleys, clamped to a carriage, turns motor rotation into long travel. The key numbers:

Travel per revolution = z · p (e.g. 20 teeth × 2 mm = 40 mm)
Steps per mm = (steps_per_rev × microsteps) / (z · p)
Belt force F = T · η / r_pitch

Compared with a leadscrew (chapter 9), a belt is fast, long-travelling, cheap and low in backlash, but not self-locking (it can be pushed back), and stretchy over very long spans or under heavy accelerating loads. It also cannot hold a vertical load without power: use a brake, a counterweight or a leadscrew for Z axes.

8.5 Chains and sprockets

A roller chain (the bicycle kind) carries far more load than a belt of the same width, tolerates dirt and heat, and does not slip. It needs lubrication, has a bit of slack, and makes noise. Basic formulas:

Sprocket pitch diameter D = p / sin(180° / z)
Chain length (links) L ≈ 2C/p + (z₁ + z₂)/2 + ((z₂ − z₁) / 2π)² · p/C
(use an even number of links to avoid an offset link)

Chordal action: a chain wraps a sprocket as a polygon, not a circle, so the chain's speed pulsates with every tooth. The speed variation is about 1 − cos(π/z) (7.6% at 8 teeth, 1.7% at 17, 0.8% at 25). Use 17+ teeth for smooth drives; small sprockets run rough and wear fast.

Printed chains. A print-in-place chain (links with 0.3–0.5 mm clearance, chapter 4) works for light loads, toys, and demonstration models, with printed sprockets. For real loads, buy a small metric or bicycle chain and print the mounts; printed links wear and stretch quickly.

8.6 Cables, capstans and tendons

A cable can only pull, never push, but it is light, flexible, and can route around corners through pulleys or a Bowden sleeve. Used for camera sliders, cable-driven robots, mechanical hands (tendons), winches, and curtain/blind mechanisms.

  • Fixed-drum drive: the cable is anchored to a drum and wound around it in a groove. Positive, no slip, precise: one turn of the drum moves the cable by the circumference. The cable has to be wound on and off in the same groove: use a helical groove for multi-turn travel.
  • Capstan (friction) drive: the cable wraps a smooth drum a few turns and is pulled by friction, with no anchor. The held tension and the pulling tension are related by T_load = T_hold · e^(μθ) (θ in radians), so a small hold force controls a large load, which is why a sailor can hold a boat with a few turns on a cleat.
  • Return: a cable can't push back, so use a second cable on an opposing drum (antagonistic pair), a spring, or a loop (a closed cable loop around two pulleys).
  • Stretch and creep: use low-stretch line (braided Dyneema/UHMWPE, steel wire) for precision; avoid nylon or cotton for precise drives. Drum and pulley diameters should be large: > 10–20× the cable diameter for fibre, much more for steel wire.
  • Anchor: cables slip out of knots in smooth plastic. Pass the cable through a hole, tie a knot larger than the hole, or use a crimp or a clamp plate. Chamfer the exit edge to stop it fraying.

8.8 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Profile and pitch of belt and pulleys match.
  • ☐ Small pulley ≥ 16 teeth (GT2) and ≥ 6 teeth in mesh.
  • ☐ A tensioning method is built in; belt runs aligned and parallel.
  • ☐ Frame stiff enough for the belt's pull (ribs, PETG/nylon).
  • ☐ Pulleys fixed to shafts with a flat and grub screw.
  • ☐ For cables: low-stretch line, large pulleys, anchored securely, return path planned.
  • ☐ Chains: ≥ 17 teeth, even link count, lubricated.
  • Designing the exact centre distance for a belt length, with no adjustment.
  • Relying on a belt to hold a vertical load.
  • Using a printed GT2 pulley with 0.2 mm resolution teeth and wondering why it skips.
  • Cranking belt tension to cure slop; it only wrecks bearings and brackets.

8.9 Check yourself

A 20-tooth GT2 pulley drives a 60-tooth GT2 pulley. What are the ratio, the pitch diameters, and travel per motor revolution if the belt were instead clamped to a carriage on the 20T pulley?

Ratio 3:1. Pitch diameters: 20·2/π = 12.7 mm and 60·2/π = 38.2 mm. Travel per revolution of the 20T pulley = 20 × 2 mm = 40 mm.

A rope with μ = 0.3 is wrapped 2 turns around a post and you hold it with 20 N. What load can it hold?

θ = 2 × 2π = 12.57 rad. e^(0.3 × 12.57) = e^3.77 ≈ 43. Load ≈ 20 × 43 ≈ 860 N. Each extra turn multiplies it by e^(0.3·2π) ≈ 6.6.

Why does a 9-tooth sprocket run rougher than a 25-tooth one?

Chordal action: the chain's effective radius varies by 1 − cos(π/z), which is ~6% at 9 teeth but only ~0.8% at 25. Fewer teeth means a rougher, noisier, faster-wearing drive.
