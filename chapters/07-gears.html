Gears

Gears are the most widely used printed mechanism and the one with the most ways to go wrong. A well-made gear pair transmits motion with a fixed ratio, silently, forever. A badly made pair clicks, binds, skips or wears to dust in a week. The difference is a few concepts (module, pressure angle, backlash, centre distance) and a handful of printing rules.

7.1 What gears do

A gear pair transmits rotation with a fixed speed ratio, and trades speed for torque (or the reverse), reverses direction, shifts an axis sideways or turns it through an angle. Unlike a belt or a friction wheel, teeth cannot slip, so the ratio is exact; that is why clocks, printers, robot joints and gearboxes use them.

The magic is the tooth shape. If the teeth are cut to the involute profile (the curve traced by the end of a string unwinding from a circle), the pair keeps a perfectly constant speed ratio even if the centre distance is slightly off. That tolerance to small centre-distance errors is exactly what makes involute gears the right choice for a 3D printer, where nothing is exactly where the CAD says it is.

7.2 The vocabulary you need

TermMeaning
Number of teeth, zTeeth on the gear.
Module, m (mm)The size of the teeth: pitch diameter ÷ teeth, m = d / z. Bigger module, bigger and stronger teeth. Two gears mesh only if they have the same module and pressure angle. (Imperial equivalent: diametral pitch, DP = z / d in inches.)
Pitch circle / diameterThe imaginary circles of the two gears that roll on each other. d = m·z.
Centre distance, aa = m·(z₁ + z₂) / 2. This is the number you most need to get right.
Ratio, ii = z₂ / z₁ (driven over driving). i > 1 is a reduction: slower and stronger.
Pressure angle, αThe angle of the force direction between teeth. 20° is the modern standard (14.5° is old). Higher angle = stronger, thicker-rooted teeth, and fewer teeth possible without undercut.
Addendum / dedendumTooth height above and below the pitch circle: normally 1·m and 1.25·m.
BacklashThe deliberate gap between the non-driving flanks. Necessary so the teeth don't jam; it is the free rotation you feel when you reverse direction.
Contact ratio, εThe average number of tooth pairs in contact. Needs to be above about 1.2 for smooth operation: at 1.0 the load transfers abruptly from one pair of teeth to the next.
UndercutOn a gear with too few teeth, the root below the base circle is cut away, weakening the tooth. At 20° pressure angle this starts below about 17 teeth.
Face width, bThe thickness of the gear (along its axis). Strength is proportional to it.

7.3 A catalog of gear types

TypeWhat it doesPrinting notes
SpurStraight teeth, parallel shafts. Simplest, most efficient (~95%+).Prints flat, perfectly. Slightly noisy. The default choice.
HelicalTeeth at an angle (helix, 15–30°). Smoother, quieter, stronger; creates axial thrust.Prints well without supports in most cases. Herringbone (double helical) cancels thrust and is a favourite for printed gearboxes.
Rack and pinionRotation ↔ straight line: travel per turn = π·m·z.Easy to print; long racks warp, so print in segments.
Internal (ring) gearTeeth on the inside; the pinion runs inside. Same rotation direction, compact.Needs more precise centre distance. The basis of planetary drives.
BevelChanges the shaft axis, usually by 90°.The apex of both cones must coincide: tricky to print and mount. Often better bought. Straight bevel works with careful orientation or supports.
Worm and wheelVery high ratio in one stage (e.g. 30:1), axes at 90°, can self-lock.Low efficiency (30–60%), heat, and strong axial thrust. Best: a steel or brass worm with a nylon printed wheel.
Cycloidal driveEccentric + lobed disc + pins: 10–50:1 in one compact stage.Popular in printed robotics; needs bearings on the eccentric and pins, and good tolerance.
Strain-wave (harmonic)A flexing cup meshes with a ring: zero-backlash high reduction.Very hard to print durably; mention only.

7.4 Ratios, torque, speed, and gear trains

For an ideal single stage with i = z₂/z₁: output speed = input speed ÷ i, output torque = input torque × i × η, where η is efficiency (assume ~0.9–0.95 per spur mesh for printed gears). Power in = power out (minus losses): P = T·ω.

  • An idler gear between two gears reverses the rotation direction but leaves the ratio unchanged.
  • A compound train puts two gears on one shaft: stage ratios multiply. 3:1 × 4:1 = 12:1.
  • Keep each printed stage to about 1:5 or 1:6. Bigger ratios need a tiny pinion (undercut, weak) or a huge wheel. For more, add a stage.
  • Place the strongest, widest gears at the output end of the train, because torque grows by the ratio at each stage, and the final gears carry it.

7.5 Planetary gears

A planetary (epicyclic) set puts a sun gear in the middle, several planets orbiting it on a carrier, and an internal ring gear around the outside. It is compact, coaxial (input and output on the same axis), and shares the load between planets, so a small printed set can carry a lot of torque.

Ratios depend on which member you hold still. With teeth Z_r = Z_s + 2·Z_p (ring = sun + two planets), the common setups are:

FixedInput → outputRatio
RingSun → carrier1 + Z_r / Z_s (reduction, e.g. 4:1 to 10:1) — the usual gearbox
SunRing → carrier1 + Z_s / Z_r (small reduction)
CarrierSun → ring−Z_r / Z_s (reverses direction)

Assembly condition: for N equally spaced planets to mesh simultaneously, (Z_s + Z_r) / N must be a whole number. Otherwise the planets cannot all be fitted without different tooth phasing.

7.6 How strong is a printed gear?

A tooth is a small cantilever beam (chapter 2). The classic estimate, the Lewis equation, treats it as such: the tangential force a tooth can carry is

F_t ≈ σ_allow · b · m · Y Y ≈ π·(0.154 − 0.912 / z) (20° teeth)
T = F_t · (d / 2)

Hence: strength grows linearly with face width b and with module (more precisely, with m²·z, since torque = F·r). A bigger module is the most effective way to get a stronger gear, then face width, then more teeth. The values of σallow in Simulation 1 are deliberately conservative figures for sustained use of printed plastic (bending fatigue, creep, layer anisotropy): treat the resulting torque as a working limit, not a failure load.

Besides tooth bending, a gear can fail by wear (contact stress on the flanks: printed teeth wear into a polished groove, then to dust), by heat (the plastic softens at the contact, especially at high speed), and by shear of the hub or keyway, which is often the weakest link. Good pairings: nylon on steel pinion (a metal motor pinion on a nylon wheel); nylon on nylon with a lubricant; PETG on PETG for light duty; avoid PLA on PLA under load.

7.7 Printing gears: the rules that matter

  • Module ≥ 1.0 for a 0.4 mm nozzle. A tooth at module 1 is 1.57 mm thick at the pitch circle: only about 3–4 extrusion lines. Module 1.5–2 is the sweet spot for printed gears; below 1 the teeth are mostly noise.
  • Use fewer, larger teeth (z ≥ 12–20) rather than many small ones, and avoid undercut: pinions below 17 teeth (20° pressure angle) are weak unless profile-shifted.
  • Print gears flat (teeth profile in the XY plane): the teeth are cut in the most accurate plane, and layers run along the tooth, which is the strong direction. Use 3–4+ walls and at least ~30% infill, or 100% for small gears. Use 0.12–0.2 mm layers.
  • Chamfer the bottom edge of the gear (0.3–0.5 mm) so the elephant foot does not close the root of the first layers and bind.
  • Design in backlash: 0.1–0.2 mm at the pitch circle for module 1–1.5; up to 0.3–0.4 at module 2+ printed in warping materials. Tune with a test print. Better a little loose than binding.
  • Centre distance is critical. It must be m(z₁+z₂)/2 within roughly ±0.1 mm. Mount shafts in a single printed part (a gearbox frame) or use slotted holes to adjust, not two separately aligned brackets. Shaft holes should be reamed or sized by coupon test (chapter 4).
  • Shafts and hubs. Support each shaft at both ends, ideally with bearings. Fix the gear on its shaft with a D-flat, grub screw, or a metal hub: a plain round press fit will slip (chapter 10).
  • Thin rims break. Keep ≥ 2·m of material under the tooth roots and spoke wide wheels instead of one thin web.
  • Material. Nylon or PETG over PLA for moving gears (chapter 3); lubricate with a little PTFE or lithium-free grease suitable for plastics.

7.9 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Same module and pressure angle on both gears.
  • ☐ z ≥ 17 for pinions at 20° (or a deliberate profile shift).
  • ☐ Contact ratio ≥ 1.2.
  • ☐ Backlash 0.1–0.3 mm designed in; centre distance exact to ±0.1.
  • ☐ Torque capacity estimated with a conservative σ, at module ≥ 1.5 and a sensible face width.
  • ☐ Shafts supported at both ends; the gear is fixed positively on its shaft.
  • ☐ Gears printed flat with 3+ walls; bottom chamfer applied.
  • Using a downloaded gear off the internet with unknown module and then matching it by eye.
  • Mounting two gears on separately printed brackets with no way to adjust the spacing.
  • Building a 1:20 single-stage reduction with a 7-tooth pinion.
  • Using PLA for a gear that's under constant load or warm.

7.10 Check yourself

A 12-tooth pinion drives a 36-tooth gear at module 2. What are the ratio and centre distance?

Ratio = 36/12 = 3:1. Centre distance a = m(z₁ + z₂)/2 = 2·(12 + 36)/2 = 48 mm. (Also: the 12-tooth pinion is below the 17-tooth undercut threshold; consider profile shift or a 17-tooth pinion with a 51-tooth wheel.)

A planetary gearbox has the ring fixed, a 12-tooth sun and three 12-tooth planets. What is the ratio, and can three planets be assembled?

Z_r = 12 + 2·12 = 36. Ratio = 1 + 36/12 = 4:1. Assembly: (Z_s + Z_r)/N = 48/3 = 16, an integer, so yes. With 5 planets 48/5 is not an integer: no.

You need twice the torque from a printed pinion, same ratio and size constraints as far as possible. What's the most effective change?

Increase the face width (linear with torque) and, if you can, the module. Switching to nylon or adding a second helix (herringbone) also helps. Adding teeth at the same module increases the diameter, which also raises the torque.
