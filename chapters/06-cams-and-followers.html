Cams and followers

A cam is a shaped wheel that pushes a follower through a motion you have drawn in advance: rise here, hold there, drop fast, rest. Where a linkage gives you whatever motion its geometry happens to produce, a cam lets you specify the motion first and cut the shape to match. And a 3D printer will happily make any shape.

6.1 What cams are for

  • Dwells. The follower sits still while the cam keeps turning. Linkages struggle to do this; a cam does it by making part of the profile a circle centred on the shaft.
  • Timed sequences. Several cams on one shaft run several followers in a fixed order: engine valves, vending machines, packaging, sewing machines, and every mechanical automaton and music box.
  • Arbitrary motion laws. Slow where you need precision or low force, fast where you don't.
  • Simple conversion from continuous rotation to reciprocating motion, with one motor and almost no parts.

The price is a higher pair (chapter 1): the cam touches the follower along a line or a point, not a surface, so contact stress is high and wear is the main failure mode. For printed cams, the contact design (section 6.5) matters as much as the profile.

6.2 Anatomy and vocabulary

TermMeaning
Base circleThe smallest circle on the cam, centred on the shaft. The follower is at its lowest position here.
Lift (stroke), hHow far the follower moves from lowest to highest.
Rise, dwell, returnThe phases of one revolution: the follower goes up over the rise angle β₁, rests over the dwell angle, goes down over the return angle β₂, then rests again at the bottom.
Pitch curveThe path traced by the centre of a roller follower. The real cam surface is this curve offset inward by the roller radius.
Prime circleThe smallest circle centred on the shaft that touches the pitch curve (base circle + roller radius).
Pressure angle φThe angle between the follower's direction of motion and the direction of the contact force (the normal to the cam surface). Only the cosine of it pushes the follower; the rest squeezes it sideways against its guide.

Cam types: plate (disc) cams (what most people mean; the follower moves perpendicular to the shaft), cylindrical (barrel) cams with a groove around a drum so the follower slides along the axis, face cams with a groove in a flat face (positive drive: the groove pushes both ways), and eccentric cams (a circle mounted off-centre, the simplest cam of all).

Follower types: knife-edge (a point: theoretical only, wears instantly), roller (rolls: low friction, can be bearing-mounted), flat-faced (a flat pad: handles any convex cam, no undercutting, but slides), mushroom / spherical (a rounded tip: forgiving, slides). Followers either translate in a guide or oscillate on a pivot arm, and may be offset from the shaft centre line to reduce pressure angle on the working stroke.

6.3 Motion laws: how the follower moves between positions

The most important cam decision is not the shape, it is the law of motion for each rise and return. The shape follows from it. Compare four common ones over a rise of height h across the cam angle β (with ω the cam's angular speed):

LawDisplacement s(ξ), ξ = θ/βPeak velocityPeak accelerationCharacter
Uniform (constant velocity)h·ξ1.00 · hω/β∞ (impulse at the ends)Simple, but every start and stop is a shock. Avoid at speed.
Simple harmonich/2 · (1 − cos πξ)1.57 · hω/β4.93 · hω²/β²Smooth in the middle; still a jump in acceleration at the ends if there is a dwell.
Cycloidalh · (ξ − sin 2πξ / 2π)2.00 · hω/β6.28 · hω²/β²Acceleration starts and ends at zero: gentlest on the mechanism, quietest. Higher peaks.
3-4-5 polynomialh · (10ξ³ − 15ξ⁴ + 6ξ⁵)1.88 · hω/β5.77 · hω²/β²Similar smoothness to cycloidal with slightly lower peaks.

Why it matters: the force needed to accelerate the follower is F = m·a, and acceleration scales with ω²: double the speed and the inertia force quadruples. A law with a sudden jump in acceleration also has a sudden jump in force: noise, wear, and in a printed part, cracks. Default to cycloidal or 3-4-5 for anything fast, simple harmonic for low-speed and simple, and uniform only for slow, light, forgiving mechanisms.

Spreading the same lift over a larger rise angle β lowers velocity (∝ 1/β) and acceleration (∝ 1/β²). Use as much angle as the timing allows.

6.4 Pressure angle and base circle size

For a translating roller follower on the shaft centre line, the pressure angle follows from the profile:

tan φ = (ds/dθ) / (R_prime + s) (R_prime = base circle radius + roller radius)

The steeper the rise and the smaller the cam, the larger φ. Above about 30° a translating follower starts to jam in its guide, because a large part of the force pushes it sideways and friction in the guide grows. The cure is almost always a bigger base circle, a longer rise angle, or an offset follower. Also check for undercutting: when the pitch curve bends more sharply than the roller radius, the offset surface forms a cusp and the cam cannot produce the intended motion. Fix: a bigger base circle or a smaller roller.

6.5 Followers, contact and wear

A cam is a high-stress joint: the force from the follower acts on a line contact of almost zero width, and printed plastic is soft. Choose the contact deliberately:

  • Roller on a bearing. Best for loads and life: a small ball bearing (e.g. a 623 or 624 skate-type) on an M3/M4 shoulder bolt as the roller. The plastic cam is then rolled, not scraped. Use a wide roller or cam face to spread load.
  • Printed roller on a pin. Works for light loads and toys, but the bore wears and sticks. Use nylon on a steel pin.
  • Flat or mushroom follower sliding on the cam. Simple (no bearing), but sliding friction and heat wear the surface. Use a smooth printed tip (nylon, or a different material than the cam), a little grease, and a low speed.
  • Cam thickness. Make the cam thick (8–12 mm) rather than thin, to lower contact stress (the Hertz contact stress falls with the length of the line). A pair of cams side by side is better than one thin one.

6.6 Keeping the follower on the cam

A plate cam only pushes. To make the follower come back, you need a return spring (the common option), gravity (slow, vertical, light), or a positive-drive arrangement: a groove (face/barrel cam) that grips the follower both ways, or a pair of followers on opposite sides.

The spring has to do more than push the follower down: at high speed it must supply the force to decelerate the rising follower and keep it in contact, otherwise the follower jumps off the cam when the profile starts to fall away faster than the follower can follow. The contact force is roughly N = F_preload + k·s + m·a (a is negative when the follower is decelerating). If N falls below zero, contact is lost, and the follower bangs back down: noise, wear and a missed motion.

6.7 Building a cam system

Timing diagram

For multiple cams on one shaft (automata, sequencers), draw a timing diagram first: a horizontal axis for shaft angle 0–360°, and one row per follower showing rise, dwell and return. Overlap and phase relationships are obvious in the diagram and invisible in the 3D model. Fix the phases by a key, D-shaft, or index marks on each cam.

Cam on a shaft

  • Use a D-shaft or a shaft with a flat and a set screw, or a hub with an M3 grub screw into a nut trap. A plain press fit of a printed cam on a round steel rod will slip (chapter 10).
  • The cam bore should be reamed, or placed as a vertical-axis hole in print (round and accurate).

Printing the profile

  • Print the cam flat on the bed, so the cam surface is a vertical wall with smooth, continuous layers around the profile, and the profile is cut in XY (the most accurate plane).
  • The CAD profile must be smooth: export a spline or a dense polygon (at least 360 points). A rough STL (visible facets) gives follower noise at every facet. The SVG export in Simulation 1 gives you 720 points.
  • Use 3–4 walls so the cam surface is solid wall (chapter 4); don't rely on infill near the edge.
  • Build the shape tolerances into the plan: a 0.1 mm profile error is a 0.1 mm follower error, but it is also a speed bump the follower sees every turn. Use slower speeds and bearings to hide it.
  • Cams and followers typically wear in the first hours and then stabilise: print the cam in nylon or PETG, not PLA, for any moving duty (chapter 3).

6.9 Checklist and common mistakes

  • ☐ Timing diagram drawn; motion law chosen for each segment.
  • ☐ Max pressure angle ≤ 30° (translating follower); no undercutting.
  • ☐ Inertia force at the top speed computed; spring preload covers it with margin.
  • ☐ Contact design: roller on a bearing, wide face, nylon or PETG.
  • ☐ Cam fixed to the shaft positively (D-shaft, key or grub screw).
  • ☐ Profile exported smooth; printed flat, 3+ walls.
  • Using a uniform-velocity law "because it's the simplest" and hearing every stroke.
  • Making the cam small to save plastic: pressure angle and undercutting will punish it.
  • Forgetting that the cam surface is the pitch curve offset by the roller radius.
  • Relying on a rubber band that goes slack over time as the return.

6.10 Check yourself

You double the cam speed. What happens to the follower's peak acceleration and inertia force?

Acceleration scales with ω², so it quadruples, and so does the inertia force F = m·a. The spring has to support that.

The follower jams in its guide mid-rise. Which single design change helps most?

Increase the base circle radius (or lengthen the rise angle): this lowers the pressure angle, so less of the force pushes the follower sideways.

Why prefer cycloidal over simple harmonic motion for a fast printed mechanism?

Cycloidal acceleration starts and ends at zero, so there are no sudden force steps at the start and end of each stroke (SHM jumps from 0 to peak acceleration at the ends when a dwell follows). That lowers noise, wear and fatigue, at the cost of a slightly higher peak acceleration.
